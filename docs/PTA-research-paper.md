# The Passage of Time Assessment

## How do you move through time? A framework built on temporal direction and temporal self-continuity

---

Time is the medium every life moves through. Some people face that medium backward, toward roots and memory and what has been. Others face it forward, toward goals and possibility and what will be. And independently of where they face, some move through time as one continuous thread, experiencing themselves as the same being across the years, while others move as a fluid sequence, comfortable letting the self reshape as each season passes.

The Passage of Time Assessment maps you onto those two dimensions: where you face in time, and how you move through it as a self. From those two coordinates, it places you in one of four modes, each representing a different relationship with time passing. Every position is valid. The test is built on specific, full-text-verified research on time perspective, temporal self-continuity, and narrative identity. This article explains what the test measures, why it was designed the way it was, what each result means, and what the research actually says about each piece of the framework.

---

## What the test measures

Two dimensions, each scored from 0 to 100. Neither is better than the other. Together they place you somewhere on a plane.

**Temporal Direction** is where your attention and value sit across time: anchored in the past or oriented toward the future. The concept comes from Philip Zimbardo and John Boyd's (1999) Zimbardo Time Perspective Inventory, the most-validated measure of individual differences in time perspective, confirmed across 24 countries by Anna Sircova and colleagues (2014) in a study of 12,200 people. Zimbardo and Boyd defined time perspective as the often nonconscious process by which people assign the flow of personal and social experience to temporal categories, past, present, and future, that give order and meaning to events. The ZTPI measures five factors: Past-Positive, Past-Negative, Present-Hedonistic, Present-Fatalistic, and Future.

The PTA draws the past pole from ZTPI Past-Positive and the future pole from the ZTPI Future factor. It deliberately does not use Past-Negative (bitter rumination, regret) as a pole, because a result that told someone their orientation toward the past is primarily a disposition toward bitterness would violate the affirming-by-design principle shared across the suite. This is a measurement ceiling the test states rather than hides: a past-anchored result does not distinguish "warmly rooted" from "stuck in difficult memories."

That decision is grounded in the actual factor structure, not made in defiance of it. Sircova's cross-cultural data show that Past-Positive and Past-Negative are only weakly related (r = −.20), and that they relate to the future in opposite directions: Future correlates positively with Past-Positive (r = +.26) and barely with Past-Negative (r = −.06). Treating "the past" as one thing is a simplification the literature does not support. The PTA builds its axis from the positive factors and reports past and future engagement as separate facet scores, so the information is not lost, just not collapsed into a single ambiguous number.

The PTA labels the high end *Future* and the low end *Past*. "Past" is not a deficit. People anchored in the past are often deeply rooted, loyal to their lineage, and carriers of memory and meaning. There is no right answer on this axis, only a description of where your attention sits across time.

**Temporal Mode** is how your self moves through time: as one continuous being or as a fluid sequence of revisable selves. This dimension draws from two research traditions that converge on the same question: do you experience yourself as continuous across time, and do you hold your life as one coherent story?

The most directly validated construct is temporal self-continuity, the perceived connection between your present self and your future (and past) selves. Hal Hershfield, Tess Garton, Kacey Ballard, Gregory Samanez-Larkin, and Brian Knutson (2009) developed a self-report index of future self-continuity built from four dimensions: similarity, connectedness, caring, and liking. In three experiments they showed that people who experience more continuity with their future selves save more, discount future rewards less, and describe their present and future selves in more overlapping terms, even after controlling for age and education. Hershfield (2011) synthesized the broader construct: temporal self-continuity transforms how people make decisions that unfold over time, and it is a measurable, consequential individual difference, not just a philosophical puzzle about personal identity.

The second tradition is narrative identity. Dan McAdams, Jeffrey Reynolds, Martha Lewis, Allison Patten, and Phillip Bowman (2001) defined two core narrative sequences that appear in how people tell their life stories: redemption sequences, in which a bad scene gives way to a good one and the bad is redeemed, and contamination sequences, in which a good scene is spoiled by what follows. Their studies of midlife adults and college students found that redemption sequences predict higher psychological well-being and contamination sequences predict lower well-being, and that redemption predicts well-being more strongly than the overall emotional tone of the narrative. Jonathan Adler and colleagues (2015) then showed in two longitudinal studies that narrative themes, agency, communion, redemption, and contamination, measured at one point in time predict trajectories of mental health over years. The narrative you tell about your life is consequential.

There is a measurement problem here that has to be stated plainly. McAdams and Adler code redemption, contamination, agency, and communion from open-ended life-story interviews. There is no validated Likert-scale equivalent for those themes. The PTA's Narrative Coherence facet adapts the underlying construct (holding your life as one intelligible, meaningful story) into self-report scenarios. It is a designer facet, built on a consequential construct but not a validated instrument in its own form. This is stated openly rather than implied.

The PTA labels the high end *Continuous* and the low end *Fluid*. "Fluid" does not mean incoherent or unstable. People who score low on continuity and coherence are often free in a way that continuous people are not: unbound by a fixed story, willing to let the self reshape, honest about how much they change. The PTA's contribution is combining Temporal Direction and Temporal Mode into a two-dimensional space, because someone who faces the past as one continuous thread is a different person from someone who faces the past as a fluid sequence of retold memories. The combination is what makes the four modes distinct.

## Sources rejected

Not every theory that sounds relevant holds up under scrutiny. Three clusters merit explanation for why they were not used as structural axes.

**Monochronic and polychronic time** is the proposal's original Axis 2. Edward Hall (1959) introduced the distinction: monochronic people prefer clock-time, scheduling, and doing one thing at a time; polychronic people prefer event-time, doing multiple things at once, fluid and contextual. The construct is real and has been validated, notably by Bluedorn (1999) through the Inventory of Polychronic Values. It was rejected as a structural axis for three reasons. First, it is an operational task-organization construct: it describes how people schedule tasks, not how they experience time passing. It does not answer "how do you move through time." Second, it confounds with Temporal Direction: people who prefer to schedule are also people who plan ahead, which is exactly what the Future pole of Axis 1 measures. Third, it confounds with Conscientiousness. Maciej Stolarski and Gerald Matthews (2016) report that the ZTPI Future factor correlates r = .60 with Conscientiousness, and a mono/poly axis would re-measure variance the suite already captures through Test 2's deliberate pole. A polychronicity axis would be redundant, operational, and Conscientiousness-loaded. It is the wrong construct for the theme.

**Consideration of Future Consequences (CFC)** is the most validated alternative for a single future-oriented axis. Alan Strathman and colleagues (1994) developed the scale, and Jeff Joireman, Monte Shaffer, Daniel Balliet, and colleagues (2012) refined the 14-item version into its two-factor structure: how far ahead you consider the consequences of your behavior (CFC-Future) versus how much you live in the immediate (CFC-Immediate). It was rejected because CFC is decision-theoretic. It measures a decision horizon: how far ahead you weigh consequences when you make a choice. That is a valuable construct, but it reduces the passage-of-time theme to a planning-horizon instrument and breaks tonal parity with the other three tests in the suite, which all measure experiential relationships, not decision weights. CFC belongs in the decision-making literature, not in a test about how you move through time.

**Narrative identity in its canonical interview form** is the most thematically resonant construct available, and it was not used as a structural axis for a single reason: measurement format. The redemption, contamination, agency, and communion themes are coded by trained raters from lengthy life-story interviews. There is no validated Likert equivalent. This is the same wall that rejected compartmentalization (Showers, 1992) and self-complexity (Linville, 1987) in the identity test earlier in the suite: the construct is real and consequential, but the method cannot be adapted to a self-report questionnaire without becoming a designer claim. Narrative identity is used as the construct basis for the Narrative Coherence facet, adapted into self-report, with the adaptation stated openly. It is not used as a structural axis.

## The four modes

Each mode is a specific combination of temporal direction and temporal mode. The profiles are archetypes, patterns that emerge from the axis geometry, not empirical clusters discovered by fitting a model to data. The test reports continuous scores and a type label; the type is the interpretation, not the measurement.

### 📜 The Archivist (Past + Continuous)

You hold your roots as one stable thread and your life as a single, ordered story. You preserve, and you keep.

**The archetype.** You move through time backward-facing and steady. Where you have been matters as much as where you are going, and you carry it all as one continuous thread. Your memories are not decoration; they are the ground you stand on. You know your roots, you keep what matters, and you order your life so the past is not lost to the present.

You experience your life as a single, coherent story. The person you were years ago is unmistakably still you, grown. The chapters connect. The steadiness runs deep: you are not reinvented by every season, you are continued by it. People come to you for context, for the long view, for the sense that something endures.

**Strengths.** Steadiness and depth. Loyalty and fidelity. A long view that sees patterns and context others miss. Wisdom drawn from memory. The ability to hold a group, a family, a project together across time.

**Blind spots.** Holding on past the point of usefulness. Reading the future through the past and missing what is genuinely new. Equating change with loss.

**Growth.** Let some chapters close. Release is not betrayal. Make room for the unfamiliar. The story is allowed to take a turn you did not plan.

**The complementary mode:** 🌱 The Pioneer. The Pioneer's comfort with reinvention, with letting the self reshape as the future unfolds, is the motion your steadiness needs. Continuity without renewal calcifies.

---

### 🧭 The Navigator (Future + Continuous)

You chart one continuous course forward, carrying a coherent sense of where you are headed. You plan, and you pursue.

**The archetype.** You move through time forward-facing and steady. The horizon pulls you, and you know where you are going. Your life has direction, and you carry yourself toward it as one continuous being: the person you are becoming is already legible in who you are now. You are not drift. You are trajectory.

What sets you apart is that your future orientation is not restless. You are pulled forward, but the pull is grounded in a coherent sense of self and story. You can describe where you are headed and how this choice fits the larger arc. You make the future legible, for yourself and for others.

**Strengths.** Vision and follow-through. Coherence between actions and identity across time. Resilience grounded in direction. Trustworthiness on long horizons.

**Blind spots.** Over-planning. Reading the future so tightly that you miss what the present offers. Confusing your plan with your purpose. Letting the horizon shrink the present to a waiting room.

**Growth.** Leave room to be surprised. Let the present be a place to live, not only a step on the way.

**The complementary mode:** 🗣️ The Storyteller. The Storyteller's looseness with the past, the willingness to hold memory as material to be remade, is the flexibility your direction needs. A course held too tightly becomes a rut.

---

### 🗣️ The Storyteller (Past + Fluid)

You weave the past into a living, revisable present, holding memory loosely and retelling as you grow. You remember, and you remake.

**The archetype.** You move through time backward-facing and fluid. The past is alive for you, but you do not keep it under glass. You carry it as material, retelling it, reworking it, finding new meaning in old scenes as the years change you. Memory is not a fixed archive in you; it is a living story you are always telling again.

Your sense of self shifts across the years, and you are honest about that. The person you were is real, but no longer quite you, and that is not a failure. It is how you grow. You hold the past without being held by it. Where The Archivist preserves one continuous thread, you weave and reweave, letting each season retell the last.

**Strengths.** Meaning-making. Freedom from a fixed story. Emotional depth drawn from a rich, revisable past. Honest self-revision.

**Blind spots.** Rewriting the past until the truth of it blurs. Losing the throughline. Others feeling the ground shift under them. Mistaking a good retelling for real change.

**Growth.** Hold the core steady even as the telling shifts. Let the present be more than material for a future story. Meet the Navigator's forward direction.

**The complementary mode:** 🧭 The Navigator. The Navigator's forward direction and coherent arc is the spine your reflection needs. Memory without trajectory circles.

---

### 🌱 The Pioneer (Future + Fluid)

You move into the new on many emerging threads, comfortable letting the self reshape as the future unfolds. You explore, and you become.

**The archetype.** You move through time forward-facing and fluid. The future calls, and you answer it on many fronts at once, willing to become someone you cannot yet name. You are not bound to a single plan or a single self. You are drawn to what is emerging, what is possible, what has not been tried, and you trust yourself to reshape as you go. For you the past is closed context, not living material: it explains how you arrived, not where you are going.

This is not restlessness, though it can look like it. It is a genuine relationship with the unknown as a place to live. You experience yourself as in-motion: the person you will be is genuinely open, and that openness is where you do your best work.

**Strengths.** Initiative and exploration. Adaptive selfhood. Comfort with uncertainty. Renewal after change. Vision that is genuinely open, not pinned to one outcome.

**Blind spots.** Leaving things unfinished. Losing the thread of who you are across too many pivots. Underestimating the value of the past and of continuity. Confusing motion with progress.

**Growth.** Finish something. Carry a throughline. Meet the Archivist's rootedness. The past is not only ballast; it is ground.

**The complementary mode:** 📜 The Archivist. The Archivist's rootedness and continuity is the ground your motion needs. Possibility without ground scatters.

---

### 🌅 The Horizon (the boundary condition)

You live near the present edge where past and future meet. You are not strongly pulled backward or forward. You are here.

Not everyone lands in a quadrant. Respondents near the center of the plane read as The Horizon, the boundary condition. Two different kinds of people arrive here, and the facet signature tells them apart.

Some are genuinely balanced: they hold their roots and their horizons in proportion, neither dwelling nor racing, and the present is where they do their living.

Others are present-focused: neither the past nor the future pulls them hard, because the only time they fully inhabit is this one. This matters, because the dominant time-perspective instrument has a known gap here. Jonte Vowinckel, Gerben Westerhof, Ernst Bohlmeijer, and Jeffrey Webster (2015) built a present-eudaimonic time perspective scale precisely because ZTPI's two present factors are not unequivocally positive: Present-Hedonistic is linked in some studies to risky behavior, and Present-Fatalistic is negative. The PTA does not claim to measure present engagement as a validated pole. It reads present-focused respondents as the Horizon, affirming what they are rather than claiming more than the construct supports.

There is a third case the engine has to handle correctly. Some respondents land near the center not because they are balanced or present-focused, but because they are intensely engaged with both the past and the future at once. Their Axis 1 score cancels to the middle even though their temporal life is full, not moderate. The test flags this as facet tension and rewrites the narrative: read the facet signature, not the axis mean. This distinction is structural. A person holding all of time at once is not the same as a person at rest in the present, and the test says so.

## How the questions were designed

The PTA has 32 questions, 8 for each of four facets, two facets under each axis. This is not an arbitrary number. The NEO-PI-R uses 8 items per facet. Under the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula, 8 items per facet gives expected reliability in the .85 to .92 range. A 16-item short form (PTA-16) is also offered, drawing a curated 4 items per facet from the same bank, landing in the .60 to .70 screening-tier band.

### The four facets

**Axis 1, Temporal Direction. A1 Past Engagement** measures how warmly and meaningfully you relate to your roots, memory, heritage, and where you have been. Items ask about felt connection to the past, gratitude for what shaped you, and how much of your sense of self comes from your history. The content draws from ZTPI Past-Positive and from Jeffrey Webster's (1993) Reminiscence Functions Scale, which validated seven functions of thinking about the past: adaptive ones like Identity and Problem-Solving, Teach and Inform, and Death Preparation, alongside maladaptive ones like Bitterness Revival and Boredom Reduction. The PTA builds the past pole from the adaptive functions only, so that being past-anchored reads as rootedness, never as bitterness or being stuck. Gerben Westerhof, Ernst Bohlmeijer, and Webster (2010) mapped which reminiscence functions support mental health and lifespan development, which informed the pole design.

**A2 Future Engagement** measures how much you are pulled toward goals, possibility, and what will be. Items ask about anticipatory engagement, hopes and dreams, and how much of your energy goes into what is coming. The content draws from ZTPI Future and from the Future-Positive factor that Stolarski and Matthews (2016) document as part of the six-factor extension of the ZTPI. Future-Negative (anxious dread about what is coming) is excluded by the same affirming-by-design logic that excludes Past-Negative.

**Axis 2, Temporal Mode. B1 Temporal Self-Continuity** measures the perceived connection between your present self and your past and future selves, drawing directly from Hershfield's four validated dimensions: similarity, connectedness, caring, and liking, extended to both temporal directions. Items ask whether the person you were ten years ago feels like the same core self, whether you feel a strong connection to the person you will be in ten years, how much you care about your future self's well-being, and how steadily your sense of self holds across the seasons of your life.

**B2 Narrative Coherence** measures whether you hold your life as one intelligible, meaningful story or as distinct, self-standing seasons, adapting the McAdams and Adler tradition into self-report. Items ask whether the story of your life has a clear shape, whether you can trace a thread from beginning to now, whether the events of your life fit into a larger meaning you can name, and whether your different life chapters form one continuous narrative or are distinct seasons each whole in itself. This facet is a designer adaptation of an interview-coded construct, and that limitation is stated on the results screen.

All 32 items use behavioral-scenario framing. Each presents a specific situation and two opposing anchor statements on a seven-point scale. The poles alternate randomly so you cannot default to one side. The bipolar format itself handles acquiescence bias. The PTA uses the same format as the other tests in the suite, which every validated time-perspective and self-continuity instrument also uses. The ZTPI is a 56-item Likert questionnaire. Hershfield's self-continuity items are seven-point Likert scales. Webster's Reminiscence Functions Scale is a 43-item self-report instrument.

## How scoring works

Scoring is hierarchical, with one construct-specific wrinkle that matters.

Within each facet, your responses are averaged and scaled to 0 to 100. Your two facet scores on each axis are then combined to produce your axis score. Axis 2 is straightforward: Temporal Mode is the mean of Self-Continuity (B1) and Narrative Coherence (B2), where 0 is Fluid and 100 is Continuous.

Axis 1 is not a simple mean, and the reason is structural. A1 (Past Engagement) and A2 (Future Engagement) are opposite poles of the same axis by design. A person high on past engagement faces backward; a person high on future engagement faces forward. So Axis 1 is computed as the mean of (100 minus A1) and A2, where 0 is Past and 100 is Future. This reverse-keying is the same kind of direction-normalization that any bipolar axis requires; it just has to be applied to the past facet rather than folded into the mean.

Your position is plotted on a two-dimensional plane with center at (50, 50). The four quadrants determine your mode. Your distance from center, prototypicality, tells you how clearly you embody your mode. Far from center means you are strongly that mode. Close to center means you are between types, and the test reports The Horizon, the boundary condition named for the present edge where past and future meet.

The test also checks for facet tension, and here the construct demands something the other tests in the suite do not. In the affect and identity tests, facet tension means two facets on the same axis pull in opposite directions and cancel each other. That logic works when both facets point the same way on the axis, so a high on one and low on the other means a genuine internal tug-of-war. But Axis 1 of the PTA is built from opposite poles. A high on A1 and a high on A2 does not mean you are internally torn; it means you are intensely engaged with both past and future at once. The PTA flags that as temporal fullness, a distinct kind of tension where the mid-axis score comes from fullness, not moderation. And critically, it does not flag the present-focused case. A person low on both A1 and A2 is genuinely present-oriented and reads as the affirming Horizon, not as tense. The same midpoint, two completely different meanings, and the test distinguishes them. Axis 2 uses the standard opposite-signs tension rule, because B1 and B2 both point toward continuity.

The facet signature is reported alongside the type, distinguishing which facet drove which axis score. A past-anchored result might read "anchored in the past through strong past engagement, with little pull toward the future." A continuous result might read "continuous mostly through self-continuity, less through narrative coherence." This is the depth that separates the PTA from a single-number score.

## The research in one place

The PTA draws on a specific body of work, verified against full-text PDFs.

**Zimbardo and Boyd (1999)** developed the Time Perspective Inventory and established time perspective as a stable individual difference with a five-factor structure: Past-Positive, Past-Negative, Present-Hedonistic, Present-Fatalistic, and Future. The PTA uses Past-Positive and Future as the poles of Axis 1.

**Sircova and colleagues (2014)** assessed the structural equivalence of the ZTPI across 26 samples from 24 countries (N = 12,200), confirming the five-factor structure holds cross-culturally with 36 invariant items and mean subscale alphas of .60 to .77. The study's latent-factor correlation matrix drives the honesty note on compositing: Past-Positive and Past-Negative are only weakly related (r = −.20), and they relate to the future in opposite directions.

**Stolarski and Matthews (2016)** tested the incremental validity of time perspective over the Big Five in predicting life satisfaction and mood (N = 265). Time perspective predicted an additional 13.7 percent of variance in life satisfaction beyond the Big Five, confirming time perspective is genuine variance not reducible to personality. The study also documents that Future correlates r = .60 with Conscientiousness and that a six-factor extension splits Future into Future-Positive and Future-Negative, which grounds the PTA's decision to build Axis 1 from the positive factors.

**Hershfield, Garton, Ballard, Samanez-Larkin, and Knutson (2009)** developed and validated a self-report index of future self-continuity built from similarity, connectedness, caring, and liking. In three experiments, higher future self-continuity predicted reduced temporal discounting, more overlap between present and future self-descriptions, and greater lifetime saving, controlling for age and education. This is the core anchor for Axis 2, facet B1.

**Hershfield (2011)** synthesized the future self-continuity construct and its behavioral consequences across intertemporal choice, establishing that perceived temporal continuity of the self is a measurable, consequential individual difference.

**McAdams, Reynolds, Lewis, Patten, and Bowman (2001)** defined redemption and contamination sequences in life narrative and showed redemption predicts well-being more strongly than overall narrative affect. This is the construct basis for Axis 2, facet B2.

**Adler and colleagues (2015)** showed in two longitudinal studies that narrative themes (agency, communion, redemption, contamination) predict trajectories of mental health over years, establishing that the narrative-identity constructs are consequential.

**Webster (1993)** developed the Reminiscence Functions Scale, a 43-item self-report instrument yielding seven functions of thinking about the past. The adaptive functions (Identity and Problem-Solving, Teach and Inform, Death Preparation) inform the past pole of Axis 1, and the maladaptive functions (Bitterness Revival, Boredom Reduction) are explicitly excluded.

**Westerhof, Bohlmeijer, and Webster (2010)** reviewed theory and evidence mapping reminiscence functions to mental health and lifespan development, guiding pole design so the low and high poles of past engagement are both affirming.

**Vowinckel, Westerhof, Bohlmeijer, and Webster (2015)** developed a present-eudaimonic time perspective scale, demonstrating that the ZTPI lacks a positive-present factor. This grounds the honesty note that the PTA does not measure present orientation as a validated pole and the affirming reframing of present-focused respondents as The Horizon.

**Sobol-Kwapinska and Jankowski (2015)** argued for a third present orientation (active concentration, the Carpe Diem scale) beyond hedonism and fatalism and linked balanced time perspective to positive orientation. This corroborates the affirming reframing of the present.

**Carstensen (2021)** synthesized socioemotional selectivity theory, a lifespan theory of motivation grounded in the perception of time horizons: when time feels expansive, people prioritize exploration and information gathering; when time feels limited, they prioritize emotionally meaningful goals. This informs the result profiles' texture on how perceived endings reshape temporal priorities.

Other sources were reviewed and rejected. Monochronic and polychronic time (Hall, 1959; Bluedorn, 1999) was rejected as operational and Conscientiousness-loaded. Consideration of Future Consequences (Strathman and colleagues, 1994; Joireman and colleagues, 2012) was rejected as decision-theoretic. Narrative identity in its canonical interview form was rejected for measurement format, the same standard applied to compartmentalization and self-complexity in the identity test, and used only as the construct basis for the designer Narrative Coherence facet.

Gosling, Rentfrow, and Swann (2003), Chang (1994), Spearman-Brown, Sun, Schmidt, and Henry (2025), McCrae and Costa (1989), and Schulte and colleagues (2020) ground the response-format decisions, inherited from the suite's first test.

## The test in practice

The PTA takes about 8 to 10 minutes for the standard 32-item form, or 4 to 5 minutes for the 16-item short form. Results are immediate: your mode, your phase within that mode, your prototypicality, and your facet signature. The short form places you reliably in a mode, but facet-level detail is rougher. Four items per facet lands in the .60 to .70 alpha band, which is stated on the result screen.

The test is designed for reflective self-discovery. It is not a clinical instrument. It cannot diagnose anything. It should not be used for hiring, retirement planning, relationship matching, or any high-stakes real-world application. A short disclaimer stating this accompanies every result.

Five limitations deserve explicit mention.

First, time perspective and temporal self-continuity both shift with life circumstances, age, and perceived endings. Laura Carstensen's (2021) socioemotional selectivity theory documents that the perception of time horizons reshapes motivation across the lifespan. The PTA captures current temporal orientation, not a pure trait. A result today may differ from a result in a different season of life, and that is a feature of the construct, not a flaw in the instrument.

Second, Axis 1 composites two separable ZTPI factors (Past-Positive and Future) and excludes the negative-valence past (Past-Negative). The axis score is a directional summary, not a claim that past and future are a single bipolar trait, and not a measure of rumination or regret. A past-anchored result does not distinguish "warmly rooted" from "stuck in difficult memories."

Third, the ZTPI has no validated positive-present factor (Vowinckel and colleagues, 2015). Present orientation is handled as an affirming boundary condition, not as a validated axis pole.

Fourth, B2 Narrative Coherence is a self-report adaptation of an interview-coded construct (McAdams and colleagues, 2001; Adler and colleagues, 2015). It is a designer facet, not a validated instrument in its own form. If pilot data ever shows B1 and B2 converging to a single factor, the separate reporting is harmless.

Fifth, Axis 1 shares substantial variance with Conscientiousness (Stolarski and Matthews, 2016, r = .60), and Temporal Mode's B1 shares conceptual space with the identity domain measured elsewhere in the suite. The cross-suite overlap is real and stated, not eliminable.

The PTA has not been validated through factor analysis, test-retest reliability assessment, or convergent and discriminant validity studies. Its scores have no population norms. A companion simulation script verifies scoring-engine self-consistency against synthetic respondents, including the construct-critical case where temporal fullness and present-focus both land at the axis midpoint but only one flags tension. That is self-consistency verification, not validation. Synthetic respondents have no psychology.
