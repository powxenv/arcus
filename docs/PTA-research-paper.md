# The Passage of Time Assessment

## How do you relate to time? A framework built on three independent temporal-engagement factors

---

Time is the medium every life moves through. Some people are anchored in where they came from, carrying their roots as a living thing. Some live fully in the present, for whom the ordinary is alive. Some are pulled forward, oriented toward horizons and possibility. And most people engage more than one of these zones, in patterns the time-perspective literature has studied for decades.

The Passage of Time Assessment measures how you engage with the three temporal zones: the past, the present, and the future. It does not force them into a single line or a two-axis grid, because time perspective is not a single dimension or a plane. It is three distinct factors, confirmed across 24 countries, each scored independently. From your three scores, the assessment places you in one of eight temporal types. Every position is valid. The test is built on specific, full-text-verified research on time perspective, and this article explains what it measures, why it was designed the way it was, and what the research actually says.

---

## What the test measures

Three independent factors, each scored from 0 to 100. None is better than the others. Together they describe which temporal zones you engage with.

**Past-Positive engagement** is your warm, rooted relationship with where you have been: your roots, your memory, your heritage, the people and places that shaped you. The construct comes from the Zimbardo Time Perspective Inventory (Zimbardo & Boyd, 1999), the most-validated measure of individual differences in time perspective. Zimbardo and Boyd defined time perspective as the often nonconscious process by which people assign the flow of experience to temporal categories, past, present, and future, that give order and meaning to events. Their Past-Positive factor captures a sentimental, warm view of the past. Anna Sircova and colleagues (2014) confirmed the factor structure across 24 countries in a study of 12,200 people, with the Past-Positive subscale showing acceptable reliability (α = .66).

The Past-Positive factor draws additional content from Jeffrey Webster's (1993) Reminiscence Functions Scale, which validated seven functions of thinking about the past. Three are adaptive (Identity and Problem-Solving, Teach and Inform, Death Preparation) and two are maladaptive (Bitterness Revival, Boredom Reduction). The PTA builds the past factor from the adaptive functions only, so that being past-engaged reads as rootedness, never as bitterness or being stuck. Gerben Westerhof, Ernst Bohlmeijer, and Webster (2010) reviewed the evidence mapping reminiscence functions to mental health, which guided this content choice.

**Present-eudaimonic engagement** is your active, meaningful relationship with the now: flow, presence, appreciation of the ordinary. This factor comes from Jonte Vowinckel, Gerben Westerhof, Ernst Bohlmeijer, and Jeffrey Webster (2015), who built and validated a present-eudaimonic time perspective scale precisely because the dominant instrument, the ZTPI, lacks a positive-present factor. The ZTPI's two present factors are Present-Hedonistic (linked in some studies to risky behavior) and Present-Fatalistic (negative). Neither is unequivocally positive. Vowinckel and colleagues' present-eudaimonic scale explained an additional 11 percent of variance in mental health beyond the other time-perspective scales. This is the validated construct that lets the PTA treat present-focus as a first-class result rather than a deficiency.

**Future engagement** is your anticipatory pull toward goals, possibility, and what will be. It draws from the ZTPI Future factor and from the Future-Positive factor documented by Maciej Stolarski and Gerald Matthews (2016), who cite the six-factor extension of the ZTPI (Carelli and colleagues) that splits Future into Future-Positive and Future-Negative. Stolarski and Matthews also report that time perspective predicts an additional 13.7 percent of variance in life satisfaction beyond the Big Five personality traits, establishing that time perspective is genuine variance, not just Conscientiousness in disguise.

The three factors are scored independently because they are independent in the data. Sircova's Table 4 cross-cultural correlation matrix shows Future correlates with Past-Positive at only r = +.26 (weakly), and the present-eudaimonic factor is largely independent of both (Vowinckel et al., 2015). Compositing them into a single axis, as an earlier version of this test did, merged weakly-related factors and produced a muddy axis score. Reporting them separately is faithful to the actual factor structure.

## What the test does not measure

This is the most important honesty point in the framework, and it is stated once at the suite level rather than scattered across notes: **the PTA measures the affirming range of time perspective only.** It cannot detect maladaptive temporal patterns.

The ZTPI has five factors. Two of them, Past-Negative (bitter rumination, regret) and Present-Fatalistic (hopelessness), are the strongest predictors of low well-being in the time-perspective literature. A third, Future-Negative (anxious dread about what is coming), documented in the six-factor extension, is similarly predictive of distress. None of these appears as a scored factor in the PTA, because a consumer self-discovery game that told a user "you are high in bitter rumination" or "your future orientation is dominated by dread" would be harmful and unethical.

The cost is real and stated: the PTA cannot distinguish "warmly rooted in the past" from "stuck in difficult memories," or "genuinely present" from "dissociated from time." A result that reads as affirming does not rule out a pattern that warrants clinical attention. Every result screen carries a one-line scope note to this effect.

This is the binding constraint of the entire suite, not just the PTA, and it is the reason the four tests cannot, even in principle, reach the validity of a clinical instrument. Stating it once, plainly, is more honest than rediscovering it in every section.

## Sources rejected

Not every temporal construct fits a consumer self-discovery test. Four clusters merit explanation for why they were not used.

**Monochronic and polychronic time** is the historical alternative for a temporal-structure axis. Edward Hall (1959) introduced the distinction: monochronic people prefer clock-time, scheduling, one thing at a time; polychronic people prefer event-time, multiple flows, fluid and contextual. The construct is real and validated, notably by Allen Bluedorn (1999) through the Inventory of Polychronic Values. It was rejected as a structural axis because it is an operational task-organization construct (it describes how people schedule tasks, not how they experience time passing), it confounds with future orientation, and it correlates heavily with Conscientiousness. A polychronicity axis would re-measure variance the suite already captures elsewhere.

**Consideration of Future Consequences (CFC)** is the most validated alternative for a single future-orientation axis. Alan Strathman and colleagues (1994) developed it; Jeff Joireman and colleagues (2012) refined the 14-item version into its two-factor structure (CFC-Future, CFC-Immediate). It was rejected because it is decision-theoretic: it measures a decision horizon (how far ahead you weigh consequences), not an experiential relationship with time. It belongs in the decision-making literature, not in a test about how you relate to time.

**Narrative identity** is the most thematically resonant temporal construct available. Dan McAdams, Jeffrey Reynolds, and colleagues (2001) defined redemption and contamination sequences in life stories; Jonathan Adler and colleagues (2015) showed these themes predict mental-health trajectories over years. But these themes are coded by trained raters from open-ended life-story interviews. There is no validated Likert equivalent. This is the same measurement-format wall that rejected compartmentalization and self-complexity in the identity test elsewhere in the suite: the construct is real and consequential, but the method cannot be adapted to a self-report questionnaire without becoming a designer claim. Narrative identity appears in the PTA as profile texture only, not as a scored factor.

**Future self-continuity** is a validated (Hershfield, Garton, Ballard, Samanez-Larkin, & Knutson, 2009) self-report construct measuring the perceived connection between your present and future self. It was considered as a fourth factor and not adopted, because an earlier version of the test extended it to the past self using a book chapter (Sani, 2010), and that extension collided item-for-item with the identity test's self-consistency items ("I still see the same core person" versus "feel like the same core self, grown" are the same question). Reverting to the future-self-only validated scale was an option, but its signal is largely captured by the Future engagement factor, so the cleaner choice was to fold it in and keep the tests distinct.

## The eight temporal types

The three factors are scored 0 to 100 each. A factor is "engaged" at or above a threshold (60 in the current design, tunable with pilot data). The pattern of engaged factors selects one of eight types.

**📜 The Keeper** (Past engaged). You carry your roots as a living thing. Memory is not decoration; it is ground. You honor what came before, and that honoring is a quiet form of love.

**⏳ The Witness** (Present engaged). You live where the past and future resolve into now. The ordinary is not ordinary to you. You meet the moment, and the moment meets you back.

**🧭 The Wayfinder** (Future engaged). The horizon pulls you. There is something ahead of you, and you can feel it. You are not waiting for life to happen; you are walking to meet it.

**🕯️ The Hearth** (Past + Present). You carry your roots into the living moment. Memory warms the now, and the now gives memory a place to land.

**🌉 The Bridge** (Past + Future). You span from roots to horizons. Where you came from and where you are going are both vividly real, and your life is the span between them.

**🌊 The Flow** (Present + Future). You move forward, fully present. Now and next are one motion for you.

**⚖️ Balanced Time Perspective** (all three engaged). All three rooms are lit. This is the pattern the time-perspective literature calls balanced time perspective (Zimbardo & Boyd, 1999; Sobol-Kwapinska & Jankowski, 2015), and it is the most consistently associated with well-being of any temporal profile.

**🍃 The Wanderer** (none strongly engaged). You are not strongly pulled by roots, presence, or horizons. You move through time lightly, unbound by any zone. The affirming surface is freedom; the honest underneath, which the scope note carries, is that low positive engagement across all three zones can also reflect disengagement worth attending to.

The type is the interpretive label. The measurement is the three continuous factor scores, always reported alongside. A respondent near a threshold is between types, and the result surfaces the dimensional scores rather than forcing a clean label.

## How the questions were designed

The PTA has 24 questions, 8 for each of three factors. This is not an arbitrary number. Eight items per factor gives expected reliability in the .85 to .92 range under the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula. A 12-item short form (4 per factor) is also offered, landing in the .60 to .70 screening-tier band.

### The three factors

Each factor is a single validated construct, measured in its native format. Items are engagement-positive statements: the respondent rates agreement from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree). This is the native format of the ZTPI and the Vowinckel present-eudaimonic scale. It is a deliberate departure from the seven-point bipolar Likert used in the affect and identity tests of the suite, because those constructs (activation, identity clarity) are genuinely bipolar, whereas the ZTPI factors are unipolar. Forcing a bipolar format onto unipolar factors creates artificial antonyms: "I love my roots" and "I focus on the future" are not opposites; they are independent.

**Factor 1, Past-Positive engagement**, samples warmth toward roots, memory, heritage, and identity. Items include "I feel a real connection to where I come from," "Memories of meaningful moments from my past bring me warmth," and "My roots are a real and living part of my identity." Content is drawn from ZTPI Past-Positive and from Webster's adaptive reminiscence functions.

**Factor 2, Present-eudaimonic engagement**, samples active, meaningful presence. Items include "I feel genuinely engaged with whatever I'm doing right now," "The present moment feels rich and full to me," and "Being fully here, now, comes naturally to me." Content is drawn from Vowinckel and colleagues' validated scale.

**Factor 3, Future engagement**, samples anticipatory pull toward goals and possibility. Items include "I feel pulled toward the years ahead of me," "Having goals to work toward is important to how I live," and "The future feels alive and full of possibility for me." Content is drawn from ZTPI Future and the Future-Positive factor.

All items are engagement-positive. Disagreement reads as "my attention is elsewhere," not as deficit. There is no reverse-keying, because there is nothing to reverse: each item asks about one clean engagement signal.

## How scoring works

Scoring is simple because the structure is honest. Within each factor, your responses are averaged and scaled to 0 to 100. The three factor scores are reported independently. There is no composite axis and no facet-tension modifier, because independent factors do not cancel.

Type assignment applies an engagement threshold (60 in the current design) to each factor. The pattern of engaged and not-engaged factors across the three selects one of the eight types. A respondent near a threshold (within 5 points) is reported as between types, with the dimensional scores surfaced rather than a forced label.

Prototypicality measures how clearly you fit your type: for single-zone types, how far the engaged factor exceeds the threshold and how far the others fall below; for multi-zone types, the mean distance of the engaged factors above the threshold. Low prototypicality means the type is a soft fit and the dimensional scores do the real interpretive work.

## The research in one place

The PTA draws on a specific body of work, verified against full-text PDFs.

**Zimbardo and Boyd (1999)** developed the Time Perspective Inventory and established time perspective as a stable individual difference with a five-factor structure: Past-Positive, Past-Negative, Present-Hedonistic, Present-Fatalistic, and Future. The PTA uses Past-Positive and Future as two of its three factors.

**Sircova and colleagues (2014)** assessed the structural equivalence of the ZTPI across 26 samples from 24 countries (N = 12,200), confirming the five-factor structure holds cross-culturally with 36 invariant items. The study's latent-factor correlation matrix drives the decision to score the three factors independently: Future correlates with Past-Positive at only r = +.26, weakly enough that compositing them produces a muddy axis.

**Vowinckel, Westerhof, Bohlmeijer, and Webster (2015)** developed a present-eudaimonic time perspective scale, demonstrating that the ZTPI lacks a positive-present factor and that the present-eudaimonic scale explains an additional 11 percent of variance in mental health. This is the core anchor for the present factor and the reason present-focus can be a first-class result.

**Stolarski and Matthews (2016)** tested the incremental validity of time perspective over the Big Five in predicting life satisfaction and mood (N = 265). Time perspective predicted an additional 13.7 percent of variance beyond the Big Five. The study documents the six-factor extension splitting Future into Future-Positive and Future-Negative, which grounds the future factor, and reports Future ↔ Conscientiousness r = .60, which grounds the cross-suite overlap honesty note.

**Webster (1993)** developed the Reminiscence Functions Scale, a 43-item self-report instrument yielding seven functions of thinking about the past. The adaptive functions inform the past factor's content; the maladaptive functions are excluded by the affirming scope limit.

**Westerhof, Bohlmeijer, and Webster (2010)** reviewed theory and evidence mapping reminiscence functions to mental health and lifespan, guiding the past factor's content so that low past engagement reads as "focused elsewhere," not "stuck in bitterness."

**Sobol-Kwapinska and Jankowski (2015)** linked balanced time perspective to positive orientation and argued for an active-concentration present beyond hedonism and fatalism. This corroborates the affirming present and names the Balanced type as a validated construct.

Other sources were reviewed and rejected. Monochronic/polychronic time (Hall, 1959; Bluedorn, 1999) was rejected as operational and Conscientiousness-loaded. Consideration of Future Consequences (Strathman and colleagues, 1994; Joireman and colleagues, 2012) was rejected as decision-theoretic. Narrative identity (McAdams and colleagues, 2001; Adler and colleagues, 2015) was rejected for measurement format (interview-coded) and used only as profile texture. Future self-continuity (Hershfield and colleagues, 2009) was considered and folded into the Future factor to keep this test distinct from the suite's identity test.

Response-format sources (Chang, 1994; Gosling and colleagues, 2003; Spearman-Brown; Sun, Schmidt, & Henry, 2025; McCrae & Costa, 1989; Schulte and colleagues, 2020) ground the format decisions, with point counts following the source instrument.

## The test in practice

The PTA takes about 6 to 8 minutes for the standard 24-item form, or 3 to 4 minutes for the 12-item short form. Results are immediate: your temporal type, your three factor scores, and your narrative. The short form places you reliably in a type, but factor-level detail is rougher (4 items per factor lands in the .60 to .70 alpha band, stated on the result screen).

The test is designed for reflective self-discovery. It is not a clinical instrument. It cannot diagnose anything. It should not be used for retirement planning, employment decisions, relationship matching, or any high-stakes real-world application. A short disclaimer stating this accompanies every result.

Four limitations deserve explicit mention.

First, the test measures the affirming range of time perspective only and cannot detect rumination, hopelessness, or anxious dread about the future. This is the binding scope limit of the suite, stated at framework level and surfaced on every result screen.

Second, the present-eudaimonic factor (Vowinckel and colleagues, 2015) is the youngest and least-replicated of the three constructs. It is the highest-variance factor, and present-leaning results carry an additional caveat to that effect.

Third, the engagement threshold (60) used to assign types is designer-set and uncalibrated. With pilot data it should be calibrated to the actual distribution.

Fourth, time perspective is partly state. Results shift with life circumstances, age, and perceived endings. Laura Carstensen's (2021) socioemotional selectivity theory documents that the perception of time horizons reshapes motivation across the lifespan. A result today may differ from a result in a different season of life, and that is a feature of the construct, not a flaw in the instrument.

The PTA has not been validated through factor analysis, test-retest reliability assessment, or convergent and discriminant validity studies. Its scores have no population norms. The type assignment and threshold are theory-informed, not empirically calibrated.
