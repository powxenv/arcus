# The Spectrum of Self Assessment

## How do you show who you are? A framework built on identity clarity and authentic self-expression

---

Pride is the courage to show who you really are. Not who you are expected to be, or who the situation asks you to be, but the person you know yourself to be when no one is watching. Some people have a clear picture of that person and express them openly. Others are still figuring it out, or they know themselves well but keep that self guarded, or they adapt to whoever they are with.

The Spectrum of Self Assessment maps you onto two dimensions of identity: how clearly you know yourself, and how closely what you show matches who you are. It also measures (v0.23) whether you have claimed that identity as yours (commitment, per Marcia 1966) and, when you opt into the informant path, how you are seen by friends on the same dimensions (visibility, per Vazire's 2010 SOKA model). From these components, it places you in one of four modes, each representing a different relationship between knowing, claiming, showing, and being-seen. Every position is valid.

---

## What the test measures

Two dimensions, each scored from 0 to 100. Neither is better than the other. Together they place you somewhere on a plane.

**Identity Clarity** is how clearly you know yourself — your attributes, your values, who you are at your core. The concept comes from Campbell, Trapnell, Heine, Katz, Lavallee, and Lehman (1996), who developed the Self-Concept Clarity Scale. Their four studies, conducted on over a thousand university students, found that self-concept clarity is a stable individual difference: some people have a well-defined, confident, internally consistent sense of who they are, while others hold more uncertain, shifting, or fragmented self-beliefs. The twelve items on Campbell's scale ask about things like whether your beliefs about yourself change frequently, whether you have a clear sense of who you are, and whether you spend time wondering what kind of person you really are. Campbell found that people with low self-concept clarity tend to score higher on Neuroticism, lower on Conscientiousness, and engage in more ruminative self-analysis — not healthy self-reflection, but a circular, anxious turning over of questions about the self. Critically, Campbell also showed that self-concept clarity is not the same thing as self-esteem, though the two are related. You can feel positively about yourself without having a clear picture of who that self is, and you can know yourself well without feeling positively about what you know.

The SSA labels the high end *Clear* and the low end *Diffuse*, but "diffuse" is not a deficit. People with a more fluid sense of self are often more open to growth, more adaptive to new contexts, and less constrained by a fixed identity. There is no right answer on this axis, only a description of how your self-concept is structured.

**Self-Alignment** is how closely your behavior and your relationships match your internal sense of who you are. This dimension draws from three research traditions that converge on the same question: do you show who you are, or do you adapt to what is expected?

The most comprehensive model comes from Kernis and Goldman (2006), who proposed that authenticity has four interrelated components: awareness (knowing and trusting your own motives and feelings), unbiased processing (evaluating yourself objectively, without denying or distorting), behavior (acting in accord with your true self), and relational authenticity (being open and genuine in close relationships). Their Authenticity Inventory, validated across multiple samples, measures all four.

Two other instruments corroborate this construct from different angles. Snyder (1974) developed the Self-Monitoring Scale, which measures the degree to which people adjust their self-presentation to fit social situations. High self-monitors are skilled at reading a room and adapting; low self-monitors behave more consistently regardless of context. Snyder's four laboratory and field studies demonstrated that self-monitoring is a stable individual difference with clear behavioral consequences — high self-monitors are better at intentionally conveying emotions but also more likely to say whatever a situation calls for, while low self-monitors are more consistent but sometimes less socially flexible.

Wood, Linley, Maltby, Baliousis, and Joseph (2008) developed the Authenticity Scale, which found three factors: self-alienation (feeling out of touch with your true self), authentic living (behaving in accord with your values), and accepting external influence (being shaped by others' expectations). Their scale showed good test-retest reliability, was invariant across samples and genders, and demonstrated that people scoring higher in authenticity also report greater subjective and psychological well-being.

The SSA labels the high end *Self-Aligned* and the low end *Other-Oriented*. "Other-Oriented" does not mean inauthentic or fake. It describes people who are attuned to social context, who adapt to fit in, who find belonging through flexibility. The SSA's contribution is combining Identity Clarity and Self-Alignment into a two-dimensional space — because someone who knows themselves well and adapts to context is fundamentally different from someone who is still figuring themselves out and also adapts to context. The combination is what makes the four modes distinct.

## Sources rejected

Not every theory that sounds relevant holds up under scrutiny. Two sources merit explanation for why they were not used.

Showers (1992) developed the concept of compartmentalization — the tendency to organize positive and negative self-knowledge into separate mental categories. The idea is that someone with a "compartmentalized" self-concept keeps their good qualities in one mental box and their bad qualities in another, while an "integrated" person mixes positive and negative self-views within the same self-aspects. The concept is relevant to identity structure and has been cited in the broader literature. The problem is that Showers's method — the card-sorting task — cannot be adapted to a self-report questionnaire. Participants literally sort cards into piles representing different self-aspects. There is no validated Likert-scale equivalent. For the SSA's questionnaire format, this source could not ground an axis.

Linville (1987) developed the concept of self-complexity — having many distinct self-aspects — and showed that greater self-complexity acts as a buffer against stress. The same measurement problem applies: the self-complexity sorting task requires participants to physically sort trait cards into groups representing different aspects of themselves. No validated self-report equivalent exists.

Tracy and Robins (2004, 2007) conducted a series of studies establishing that pride, as an emotion, has two distinct facets: authentic pride (achievement-based, prosocial, correlated with genuine self-esteem) and hubristic pride (self-aggrandizing, defensive, correlated with narcissism and shame-proneness). This is a well-validated framework with clear factor structure and distinct personality correlates. It was not used as a structural axis for two reasons. First, hubristic pride is inherently negative in its correlates. Making it one pole of a continuum would create a "bad" half of the test — a result that tells someone they experience pride primarily as arrogance and shame would violate the SSA's affirming-by-design principle. Second, pride-as-emotion measures how you feel when you succeed, not how you structure or express your identity. For a test asking "how do you show who you are?", identity clarity and self-alignment are the more direct constructs. The Tracy and Robins framework is noted here and may inform the result profiles in future iterations.

## The four modes

Each mode is a specific combination of identity clarity and self-alignment. The profiles are archetypes — patterns that emerge from the axis geometry — not empirical clusters discovered by fitting a model to data. The test reports continuous scores and a type label; the type is the interpretation, not the measurement.

### 🔦 The Beacon (Clear + Self-Aligned)

You know yourself, and you show yourself. There is little gap between who you are and what the world sees.

**The archetype.** You have a clear sense of who you are — your values, your strengths, your edges — and you express that self openly. There is no performance, no mask. What people see is what they get, and what they get is genuine. This is self-assurance without self-aggrandizement. You have done the work of knowing yourself, and you are comfortable enough to let yourself be known.

Your presence is steady, grounded, and honest. People trust you because you are consistent, not rigid, but reliably yourself across contexts. You are the person others turn to for an honest opinion, because your opinion will genuinely be yours.

**Strengths.** Integrity, self-possession, clarity of purpose. Authentic relationships built on trust. Resilience under pressure — your sense of self holds.

**Blind spots.** Being so clear about who you are that you stop evolving. Underestimating how hard it is for others to know themselves as clearly. Mistaking clarity for completeness.

**Growth.** Leave room for surprise. You are still becoming, not just being. Let others show themselves at their own pace.

**The complementary mode:** 💠 The Aurora. The Aurora's flexibility — the willingness to be shaped by context rather than always shaping it — is the adaptability your clarity needs.

---

### 🔮 The Prism (Clear + Other-Oriented)

You know yourself, but you refract differently depending on who you are with. Your inner clarity stays steady while your presentation shifts.

**The archetype.** Like a prism, you take a steady inner light and split it into different colors depending on the angle and the context. You know your values, your strengths, your identity, but you adjust your presentation to fit the social situation. This is not inauthenticity. It is social intelligence. You are attuned to the people and expectations around you, and you navigate them skillfully without losing your core.

Where The Beacon shines the same way in every room, you adapt. Your clarity gives you a home base; your adaptability lets you navigate the world effectively.

**Strengths.** Social intelligence, role flexibility, self-awareness without over-exposure. The ability to build rapport across different types of people while protecting your private self.

**Blind spots.** People may feel they do not know the real you. The effort of managing multiple presentations can be draining. Being perceived as inauthentic by those who value raw consistency.

**Growth.** Let some people see the full beam, not just the refracted light. Practice being the same person across two situations that call for different versions of you.

**The complementary mode:** 🔥 The Ember. The Ember's raw genuineness — the willingness to be seen without polish — is the depth your flexibility needs.

---

### 🔥 The Ember (Diffuse + Self-Aligned)

You do not have a fully formed sense of yourself, but what you show is genuine. The light is soft, but it is real.

**The archetype.** You are still figuring yourself out, and you are honest about it. Your sense of identity is not fully crystallized, and that uncertainty is part of who you are. But even in that openness, you do not pretend. What you show to the world matches what you genuinely feel and believe in this moment, even if that may change tomorrow.

There is an honesty in not knowing. You do not claim to have all the answers about yourself, and you do not perform certainty you do not feel. You are in process, and the process is visible. The courage here is real: to be seen while still becoming.

**Strengths.** Authenticity in uncertainty. Openness to growth and change. Emotional honesty. Freedom from the burden of maintaining a consistent self-image.

**Blind spots.** Difficulty committing to a path because you do not know who you will be down the road. Others may find you unreliable because you change too much. Vulnerability to being defined by others because you have not defined yourself.

**Growth.** Practice choosing. Commitment is part of identity formation, not the enemy of it. Let some things settle.

**The complementary mode:** 🔮 The Prism. The Prism's ability to know itself and adapt without losing its core — that is the anchor your openness needs.

---

### 💠 The Aurora (Diffuse + Other-Oriented)

Your sense of self shifts with context, and your presentation shifts with it. You are fluid, adaptive, always in motion.

**The archetype.** You are not one thing. You are many things, and which one you are depends on where you are and who you are with. Your identity is not a fixed landscape — it is a shifting aurora, beautiful and fluid, always in motion. You adapt not just your presentation but your sense of self to fit the context.

This is not emptiness. The Aurora is not a lack of self — it is a self that forms in response to context, like water taking the shape of its container. You are at home in the in-between, the not-quite-defined, the relational self that emerges differently in each genuine encounter. Where others feel pinned down by consistency, you feel freed by adaptability.

**Strengths.** Exceptional adaptability. Empathy and attunement to context. Freedom from maintaining a consistent self. Resilience through reinvention.

**Blind spots.** Difficulty knowing what you genuinely want versus what the current context suggests. People close to you may feel they never see the real you. Vulnerability to external influence.

**Growth.** Establish a core — a set of preferences or values that stay with you across contexts. Practice noticing the difference between "this is what I want" and "this is what this situation wants me to be."

**The complementary mode:** 🔦 The Beacon. The Beacon's steady self-knowledge is the still point your motion needs.

## How the questions were designed

The SSA has 32 questions, 8 for each of four facets — two facets under each axis. This is not an arbitrary number. The NEO-PI-R uses 8 items per facet. Under the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula, 8 items per facet gives expected reliability in the .85 to .92 range. A 16-item short form (SSA-16) is also offered, drawing a curated 4 items per facet from the same bank, landing in the .60–.70 screening-tier band.

### The four facets

**Axis A — Identity Clarity. A1 Self-Knowledge** measures how well you know your own attributes, values, and preferences. Items ask about the clarity of self-beliefs: whether you can describe yourself easily, whether you know what matters to you, whether introspection feels like familiar territory or unfamiliar terrain. **A2 Self-Consistency** measures the stability and internal coherence of those self-beliefs: whether your view of yourself changes day to day, whether different parts of your personality feel like they belong together, whether your best self and your worst self feel like two versions of the same person or two different people. Both draw from Campbell's Self-Concept Clarity Scale. The split into two facets is a design choice — Campbell's original instrument is unidimensional — and is noted openly in the design documentation.

**Axis B — Self-Alignment. B1 Authentic Living** measures whether your behavior matches your internal values and feelings, drawing on Kernis and Goldman's awareness and behavior components of authenticity. Items ask about how you act in social situations, whether the person in public matches the person in private, whether you stay true to what matters to you when the situation asks for something different. **B2 Relational Authenticity** measures openness and genuineness in close relationships, drawing from Kernis and Goldman's relational orientation component. Items ask about sharing real thoughts and feelings, being honest with the people closest to you, and whether you let people in fully or keep some distance.

All 32 items use behavioral-scenario framing: each presents a specific situation and asks what you would do in that situation. This is a deliberate choice grounded in the measurement philosophy of the research traditions — asking "what do you do" rather than "what are you like" produces more valid signals for identity constructs.

### The format

Each question presents a short scenario and two opposing anchor statements on a seven-point scale. The poles alternate randomly so you cannot default to one side. The bipolar format itself handles acquiescence bias. The SSA uses the same format as the other tests in the suite — seven-point bipolar Likert — which every validated identity instrument also uses. Campbell's SCC, Kernis and Goldman's Authenticity Inventory, Snyder's Self-Monitoring Scale, and Wood's Authenticity Scale are all Likert-based questionnaires.

## How scoring works

Scoring is hierarchical. Your eight responses within each facet are averaged and scaled to 0–100. Your two facet scores on each axis are then averaged to produce your axis score. For Axis A (Identity Clarity), 0 is Diffuse and 100 is Clear. For Axis B (Self-Alignment), 0 is Other-Oriented and 100 is Self-Aligned.

Your position is plotted on a two-dimensional plane with center at (50, 50). The four quadrants determine your mode. Your distance from center — prototypicality — tells you how clearly you embody your mode. Far from center means you are strongly that mode. Close to center means you are between types, and the test reports The Reflection, the boundary condition named for the surface where light meets itself — the act of seeing yourself clearly.

The test also checks for facet tension: when two facets on the same axis pull in opposite directions and cancel each other, the axis mean is misleading. A person whose behavior is highly authentic but whose relationships are guarded would land mid-axis on Self-Alignment, looking balanced when they are actually extreme in two opposite directions. The facet-tension modifier fires in these cases and rewrites the narrative from "you are balanced" to "read the facet signature — you are near center from cancellation, not from moderation."

The facet signature is reported alongside the type, distinguishing which facet drove which axis score. A high-Clarity result might be driven mostly by Self-Knowledge rather than Self-Consistency. A high-Self-Aligned result might be driven mostly by Authentic Living rather than Relational Authenticity. This is the depth that separates the SSA from a single-number score.

## The research in one place

The SSA draws on a specific body of work, verified against full-text PDFs:

**Campbell, Trapnell, Heine, Katz, Lavallee, and Lehman (1996)** developed and validated the Self-Concept Clarity Scale, finding one major factor. SCC is independent of self-esteem and predicts the stability and consistency of self-descriptions. Low SCC is associated with high Neuroticism, low Conscientiousness, chronic self-analysis, and low internal state awareness.

**Kernis and Goldman (2006)** proposed and validated a four-component model of authenticity: awareness, unbiased processing, behavior, and relational authenticity. Their Authenticity Inventory demonstrates good psychometric properties, and the components show differential relationships with well-being, self-concept structure, and interpersonal functioning. The SSA covers three of the four components; unbiased processing — objectively evaluating oneself without distortion — is the component not directly captured by self-report items.

**Snyder (1974)** developed the Self-Monitoring Scale and demonstrated that adjusting self-presentation to social context is a stable individual difference with observable behavioral consequences. High self-monitors are skilled at social adaptation but may appear less consistent; low self-monitors are consistent but less flexible.

**Wood, Linley, Maltby, Baliousis, and Joseph (2008)** developed the Authenticity Scale with three factors: self-alienation, authentic living, and accepting external influence. The scale is invariant across samples and genders, and higher authenticity is associated with greater well-being.

Other sources (Showers 1992, Linville 1987) were reviewed and rejected because their measurement formats (card-sorting tasks) cannot be adapted to a self-report questionnaire. Tracy and Robins (2004, 2007) on authentic versus hubristic pride were reviewed and set aside because the hubristic pole's negative personality correlates would violate the test's affirming-by-design principle.

Gosling, Rentfrow, and Swann (2003), Chang (1994), Spearman-Brown, Sun, Schmidt, and Henry (2025), McCrae and Costa (1989), and Schulte et al. (2020) ground the response-format decisions, inherited from Test 1.

## The test in practice

The SSA takes about 8 to 10 minutes for the standard 32-item form, or 4 to 5 minutes for the 16-item short form. Results are immediate: your mode, your phase within that mode, your prototypicality, and your facet signature. The short form places you reliably in a mode, but facet-level detail is rougher — four items per facet lands in the .60–.70 alpha band, which is stated on the result screen.

The test is designed for reflective self-discovery. It is not a clinical instrument. It cannot diagnose anything. It should not be used for hiring, employment, or any high-stakes real-world application. A short disclaimer stating this accompanies every result.

Three limitations deserve explicit mention. First, self-concept clarity and authenticity are both correlated with self-esteem. Campbell (1996) and Kernis and Goldman (2006) both document this relationship. The SSA measures identity structure and expression, not self-esteem, but the results share variance with self-esteem that the scales cannot fully disentangle. Second, the A1/A2 and B1/B2 splits are **content-sampling bins, not validated facets** (v0.22 reframe). Campbell's Self-Concept Clarity is unidimensional (one factor), so A1 (Self-Knowledge) and A2 (Self-Consistency) sample different content within one construct, retained so the facet-tension detector has two sub-scores to compare. The same applies to B1/B2 within authenticity. They are not claimed to be validated sub-factors; if pilot data shows them converging, the separate reporting is harmless because it never claimed two factors. Third, the Pride cultural movement has specific social and political meanings about marginalized identities attaining visibility. The SSA measures the universal psychological dimension of showing who you are, not the specific experience of marginalized groups. This gap is stated rather than implied. The suite-wide affirming scope limit also applies: the SSA cannot detect clinically significant identity disturbance.

**v0.23 additions.** Fourth, the commitment component (v0.23) measures whether identity is claimed or provisional (Marcia 1966), adding the claim-act to the clarity and authenticity axes. Fifth, the opt-in informant path (v0.23) enables real SOKA visibility (Vazire 2010; Beer & Vazire 2017): invited friends rate the user on the same items and the self-other agreement gap is computed. The four-component Pride profile (clarity, authenticity, commitment, visibility) is our synthesis and is unvalidated, though each component is independently validated. Without the informant path (user declines), visibility is not measured and the result carries an explicit note. Large self-other gaps can indicate low insight (Carlson, Vazire & Oltmanns 2013), which is handled under the affirming scope limit.

The SSA has not been validated through factor analysis, test-retest reliability assessment, or convergent and discriminant validity studies. Its scores have no population norms. A companion simulation script verifies scoring-engine self-consistency against synthetic respondents, but this is not validation — synthetic respondents have no psychology.
