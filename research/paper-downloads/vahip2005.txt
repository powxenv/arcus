Abstract
Background: This is a first attempt to evaluate the reliability and factor structure replicability of the Temperament
Evaluation of Memphis, Pisa, Paris and San Diego Autoquestionnaire (TEMPS-A) in its Turkish Version. The questionnaire
is a self-report 110-item measure that postulates five affective temperaments—the depressive, cyclothymic, irritable,
hyperthymic, and anxious—which embody both strengths and liabilities along affective lines. Methods: The questionnaire
was administered to 658 clinically-well subjects in a Turkish university circle. We undertook item analysis and test – retest
reliability. We then examined internal consistency through factor analysis with PCA rotation. Results: We found good to
excellent test – retest reliability (0.73 – 0.91), and internal consistency (0.77 – 0.85). We deleted 10 items with factor loading
< 0.20 for their own subscales, resulting in a questionnaire with 99 items. Despite considerable overlap between depressive
and cognitive anxiety traits, a distinct ‘‘nervous’’ – anxious factor emerged as well, and the hypothesized (original English) 5factor structure of the TEMPS-A was supported. Cut-offs for each temperament were based on z-scores higher than + 2S.D.
Dominant irritable (3.7%), nervous – anxious (3.7%) and depressive (3.1%) temperaments were the most common in this
population, whereas dominant cyclothymic (1.7%) and hyperthymic (1.2%) temperaments were relatively uncommon. These
temperaments tended to lose their intensity with age. As expected, women scored significantly higher on the nervous –
anxious, and men on the hyperthymic temperaments. Limitations: The sample was composed of younger subjects with higher
education than the general population of Turkey. Although the distribution of the scores for each of the temperaments
deviated somewhat from normal curves, for heuristic reasons we did attempt to provide prevalence rates based on z-scores.
Conclusion: In this preliminary version of the TEMPS-A, we have retained 100 (of the original 110) traits loading >0.20.
Some deleted items referred to sleep, others appeared socially desirability traits in the Turkish culture endorsed by many
subjects. Nonetheless, item analyses within each factor revealed traits indicative of personal assets (specific to each
temperament) along with those which might represent vulnerability to affective illness. This is in line with the hypothesized
original theoretical framework of the senior authors. Even in this ‘‘first pass,’’ in its Turkish version the TEMPS-A is a

1. Introduction
Although origins of the concept of temperament
can be traced back to Hippocratic times, Kraepelin
(1899) was the first to formally introduce it into
clinical psychiatry. The concept of temperament was
popular until the psychodynamic preference for
personality disorders overshadowed this classical
perspective during the second half of the 20th
century. The revival of interest in temperament in
contemporary psychiatry is relatively recent (Akiskal
et al., 1979).
This research builds on the work of the last author
(H.S.A.)—originally based in Memphis—who operationalized depressive, hyperthymic, irritable, cyclothymic and anxious temperaments as the proximal
behavioral phenotypes in the pre-morbid course of
affective disorders (Akiskal, 1989; Akiskal and Akiskal, 1992; Akiskal and Mallaya, 1987). Following
Kraepelin (1899); Kretschmer (1936), this body of
work hypothesized that these temperaments represent
the subclinical foundations of affective disorders, and
that affective temperament represents an early clue for
a recurrent mood, pre-bipolar disorder (Akiskal,
1995). As for interpersonal relationships and life
events, they have been proposed as mediating mechanisms between temperament and clinical mood episodes (Akiskal, 1996).
Temperament, character and personality are all
overlapping constructs referring to the basic nature
of a person (von Zerssen and Akiskal, 1998). Temperament is believed to be more ‘‘heritable,’’ and
relatively stable throughout life. To measure affective
traits which could be stable over the lifespan, the
senior author (H.S.A.) and his colleagues first studied
subthreshold mood and neurotic disorders, leading to
the delineation (Akiskal, 1979; Akiskal, 1983) and
operationalization (Akiskal and Mallaya, 1987; Akiskal, 1998; Akiskal and Akiskal, 1992) of affective
temperaments that could be useful in clinical prac-

tice. Subsequently, in collaboration with psychiatrists
and primary care physicians in Memphis, Pisa, Paris,
and San Diego during the early 1990s, they developed these temperaments into an interview (TEMPSI) (von Zerssen and Akiskal, 1998; Placidi, 1998),
and eventually into a self-administered Autoquestionnaire or Temperament Evaluation of Memphis, Pisa,
Paris and San Diego Autoquestionnaire (TEMPS-A)
format consisting of five subscales (Erfurth et al.,
2005). Each of the subscales consist of affect-laden
items, as well as socially adaptive traits (Akiskal and
Akiskal, 1988, 1992).
The aim of the present report is to examine the
reliability of the Turkish version of the self-report
TEMPS-A and to replicate its postulated 5-factor
structure. A related objective of ours was to provide
preliminary cut-off points of the each of the subscales,
and their relative prevalence in the Turkish population
under study.

2. Methods
2.1. Measures
The TEMPS-A is a self-report instrument consisting of five subscales. Its 110 constituent items inquire
about the subject’s whole life about traits along
depressive, cyclothymic, hyperthymic, irritable, and
anxious lines.
2.2. Translation
The questionnaire was translated into the Turkish
language by two bilingual psychiatrists. The questionnaire was back-translated into English by a third
bilingual psychiatrist. The originator of the instrument (H.S.A.) evaluated the back-translated version
of the measure, and an identical translation process
was carried out for the items that required revisions.

3. Results
3.1. Subjects
The measure was administered to 1000 subjects.
Thirty four percent of the administered questionnaires
were either incomplete or not returned. The analyses
reported herein pertain to the 658 (66%) protocols
which were complete. Completers and not-completers
were demographically comparable.

3.2. Reliability
As shown in Table 1, test –retest reliability ranged
from 0.73 to 0.93 and Cronbach-Alpha coefficients
from 0.75 to 0.84.
Item analysis indicated that 10 items had alpha
values of < 0.2 (Table 2). The homogeneity of the scale
will improve if these were removed from the scale.
Some are sleep related items, others appear to be social
desirability items. These items were deleted from
further consideration in the final 100 item questionnaire TEMPS-A, Turkish version (see Appendix A,
where these items are marked by an asterisk, *).
3.3. Validity
The construct validity of the scale was studied
using exploratory factor analysis (PCA type 2, varimax rotation). Factor analysis of all items yielded
five main factors (Appendix A). As can be seen,
factor loadings of depressive and anxious (cognitive)
items overlap significantly and form a ‘‘cluster.’’
However, another smaller factor loading with certain
somatic items of the anxious temperament stands out
as a separate cluster, without any overlap with other
temperaments, which we decided to label the ‘‘nervous temperament’’. Cyclothymic, irritable and
hyperthymic temperaments formed largely independent factors (Table 3).

The findings of this study indicate that TEMPS-A
(Turkish Version) is a reliable instrument that is
suitable for future studies in Turkey (Vahip et al.,
in press).
Overall, the hypothesized 5-factor structure of
the TEMPS was supported, each scale contributing
largely to its own factor. However, factor analysis
findings indicate that depressive and anxious (cognitive) traits overlap considerably. Although this
finding implies that depressive and anxious traits
coexist in the community, a dominant depressive
temperament—including trait cognitive anxiety—
could be delineated; we also found evidence for a
distinct ‘‘nervous’’– anxious factor. There was also
a minor overlap of the dominant hyperthymic with
several irritable traits. Dominant cyclothymic and

irritable temperaments had minimal overlap with
items from other subscales.
TEMPS-A (Turkish Version), like its original
English counterpart, has good internal consistency.
This was enhanced by removing items with low
factorial loading from the instrument. We found that
several sleep items were not useful as trait measures. Curiously, being a ‘‘short sleeper’’ did not
characterize the hyperthymic. Excessive need for
sleep characterized not the depressive, but the
cyclothymic, where it may tap the tendency to
fatigue and disposition to retarded depression. As
expected, unrestful sleep and initial insomnia characterized the anxious temperament. The lack of
traitness for some of the sleep items has been
found previously in the Italian sample as well
(Placidi, 1998). Future studies are needed to clarify
this question. Another item with weak performance
was, ‘‘I am a hardworking person’’ (originally in
the depressive scale). This item has a positive
social valence in the Turkish culture, and does
not appear to tap depressive traits. Other items
could also be influenced by cultural factors. Thus
the self-concept of being neat and organized (#18,
originally in the depressive temperament) appears
like a ‘‘social desirability’’ item only endorsed by a
positive temperament like the hyperthymic, and we
have elected to delete it as well. It is expected that
the urge to engage in outrageous acts to be characteristic of the cyclothymic (#41), yet it was
endorsed by the hyperthymic; as other items (such
as narcissistic traits) seemed to measure the same
idea, we also elected to delete it. On the other
hand, the theoretical structure of the TEMPS-A
which mixes adaptive social traits and affect-laden
items is supported by the subfactorial loading for
each temperament (Table 4). These include, among
others, fun loving, narcissism, sociability and worrying about kin.
Now brief comments about each of the constituent
temperaments. The depressive temperament has been
validated and standardized on the basis of this first
psychometric study in Turkey, yielding a prevalence
of 3.1%. In previous studies based in clinical populations (Klein, 1990; Phillips et al., 1998) using similar
criteria in other populations, much higher rates of
prevalence have been reported, possibly due to the use
of different cut-offs. Rates similar to the present one

were obtained in the study with TEMPS-I Scale in an
Italian student population (von Zerssen and Akiskal,
1998; Placidi, 1998).
The prevalence for cyclothymia in a student
population was reported as 4– 6% (Depue et al.,
1981). In the study that was done in Italy to
determine the reliability and validity of the
TEMPS-I, the prevalence of this temperament was
6.4% (Placidi, 1998). We found that the prevalence
of cyclothymic temperament in our population was
1.7%, and had a good validity. The difference can
be due to interview versus self-rated measures, or
in part explained by the much younger Italian
sample. Alternatively, there can be real population
differences.
In the Turkish sample, the prevalence of dominant
hyperthymic temperament was 1.2%, which was validated and standardized. These data cohere with two
other population studies. Thus, Eckblad and Chapman
(1986) found that intermittent hypomanic characteristics occurred in 1% in 1519 college students. Wicki
and Angst (1991) found a prevalence of 4% for
hypomania, of which one out of four (1% of the
population) was chronically hypomanic, i.e. ‘‘hyperthymic’’. However, in the study that was carried out in
Italy, none of the subjects obtained scores in the
+ 2S.D. range; it appeared as though many Italian
students were hyperthymic (between the 1st and 2nd
+ S.D.), and for that reason standardization could not be
carried out in that population. Further studies are
needed to examine the relative merits of interview
and self-rated versions in assessing the hyperthymic
temperament.
The distribution of irritable temperament was
non-Gaussian and unstandardized in the Italian
TEMPS-I study (Placidi, 1998), it was interestingly
well standardized and validated in the Turkish
sample, accounting for 3.7% of subjects. Such a
difference could stem from better operationalization
of this temperament in TEMPS-A compared with
TEMPS-I, though true population differences cannot
be ruled out.
The prevalence of the dominant ‘‘nervous’’ temperament in the Turkish sample was 3.7%. This rate is
compatible with epidemiologic data on generalized
anxiety (Wittchen et al., 1994).
Depressive, irritable and ‘‘nervous’’ temperaments
were the most common temperaments in the Turkish

S. Vahip et al. / Journal of Affective Disorders 85 (2005) 113–125

sample, and cyclothymic and hyperthymic, the least
common. As already pointed out, except for concordance with the depressive, these rates are in some
ways in sharp contrast with those from Italy. The
present findings are an important beginning, since
this is the first study in Turkey that assessed affective temperaments. Therefore, replication of this
study is needed on a larger and more representative
national sample to decide whether such differences
are due to population/cultural differences, or emanate
from methodologic factors.
As for the distribution by gender, in our population men scored significantly higher on hyperthymic
temperament, and this finding is similar with other
studies in clinical populations (Akiskal, 1984; von
Zerssen and Akiskal, 1998; Perugi, 1990). As
expected, women scored significantly higher on the
‘‘nervous’’ temperament and also had more dominant
anxious temperament traits than males.
A significant difference with regard to age groups
was not obtained, however it was observed that
extreme scores in older ages were rare. Although this
finding was not statistically significant, it implied that
as age increases, the temperament scores might decrease. Even this trend might be a measurement

artifact, given the relatively small subsample in the
elderly in the present study.

5. Conclusions and future directions
We tentatively conclude that anxious, irritable and
depressive temperaments are the most dominant
temperaments in Turkey: The ‘‘nervous’’ temperament is more frequent in females, irritable temperament in males.
This is a first attempt to psychometrically evaluate the TEMPS-A (Turkish Version). It is a reliable
and valid instrument in our hands. Since it is a selfreport instrument, it is convenient to administer it in
large epidemiological studies. In clinical and genetic
studies, however, reliability and validity of the
instrument are of greater importance, therefore, further development and standardization of semi-structured format of the 100-item TEMPS-A could
improve the quality of future studies in clinical
populations from different socio-economic classes
with different literacy rates—such as that in Turkey—where investigators often need to read the
questions to the subjects.
