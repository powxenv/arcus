POLYCHRONICITY, INDIVIDUALS,
AND ORGANIZATIONS
Allen C. Bluedorn
ABSTRACT
Polychronicity is the extent to which people prefer to be engaged in two
or more tasks simultaneously. Relationships between polychronicity and
four variables were examined in data from four samples totaling 1,173
participants. Only one statistically significant relationship occurred
between polychronicity and propensity for creativity after controlling for
other variables. Consistent significant relationships were found, however,
between polychronicity and orientation to change (positive), tolerance for
ambiguity (positive), and organizational attractiveness (positive or negative depending on whether the organization demonstrated a high or low
level of polychronicity, respectively). Concatenated replications reproduced
each of these three relationships in at least two samples.

To say that someone cannot chew gum and walk at the same time is dismissive, not just of the person’s intellect, but of the individual’s very ability
to manage life itself. For life seldom presents itself arrayed in convenient
sequences that allow a person to complete a single task at a pace of one’s
liking before beginning another. Instead, life delivers tasks in bunches,
which occur in uneven bursts, so the option to be concerned solely with one
task or event at a time seldom presents itself. This means the real choices are

about how many things to engage at once, and people’s preferences about
this choice are a phenomenon known as polychronicity.
Polychronicity is ‘‘the extent to which people (1) prefer to be engaged in
two or more tasks or events simultaneously and are actually so engaged (the
preference strongly implying the behavior and vice versa) and (2) believe
their preference is the best way to do things’’ (Bluedorn, 2002, p. 51). As
Bluedorn (2002, p. 52) notes, the concept of simultaneously in the preceding
deﬁnition can refer to literally doing two or more things at the same time,
such as chewing gum while walking, two tasks that seem to combine well, or
to talking on a cellular phone while driving, two tasks that combine poorly
(McEvoy et al., 2005; Redelmeier & Tibshirana, 1997). But simultaneously
may also refer to switching back-and-forth between several projects, often
several times, rather than working through a set of several projects serially.
As a description of fundamental individual and cultural differences, Hall
(1959) was the ﬁrst social scientist to describe this difference; he was also the
one to apply the polychronic label to it (e.g., Hall, 1983). More recently
researchers in psychology (e.g., Conte & Gintoft, 2005; Conte, Rizzuto, &
Steiner, 1999), marketing (e.g., Cotte & Ratnewshwar, 1999; Kaufman,
Lane, & Lindquist, 1991), and management (e.g., Bluedorn, 2002; Hecht &
Allen, 2005; Palmer & Schoorman, 1999; Slocombe & Bluedorn, 1999;
Tinsley, 1998) have conducted qualitative and quantitative studies of polychronicity. Indeed, two entire issues of the Journal of Managerial Psychology
and part of a third were devoted to the topic of polychronicity (Bluedorn,
1999). But this burgeoning stream of research has just begun to identify
organization science relevant variables to which polychronicity might be
related (e.g., organizational commitment, role overload, negotiation behavior, etc.). For example, Bluedorn’s recent research on a sample of dental
practices revealed that although there was no relationship between polychronicity and general life stress for nondentists in the practices, a robust
negative relationship existed for the dentists, even after controlling for
age, gender, and years with the practice (the greater the polychronicity, the
lower the life stress). And in a simple path analysis, general life stress was
strongly correlated, in turn, (negatively) with job satisfaction (Bluedorn,
2002, pp. 65–67, 281–282). The ﬁndings from these several studies all suggest
polychronicity is an important variable that characterizes the way individuals engage life—Bluedorn describes it as a ‘‘fundamental process strategy’’
(p. 48)—and important as well in the culture-carrying collectivities in which
individuals participate.
Given the success of these studies in identifying relationships between polychronicity and both individual and organizational phenomena, I conducted

a series of studies to extend these early ﬁndings and did so in a way to
promote conﬁdence in the character of the results.
Speciﬁcally, the series of three studies I report in this article continues
the earlier research efforts by examining polychronicity’s relationships with
(1) orientation to change, (2) propensity for creativity, (3) tolerance for
ambiguity, and (4) judgments of organizations as attractive or unattractive
places to work. In conducting this series of studies, I developed new methods for studying polychronicity and also tested several methodological
issues associated with these methods. In doing so, it is my hope that the
results of these tests would provide a more rigorous methodological examination of the potential ﬁndings and, therefore, produce greater conﬁdence
in the results.
In what follows I will present the three studies in the order in which they
were conducted. Part of the reason for doing this is that some longitudinal
connections exist between some of the studies, so this chronological ordering
helps simplify their presentation. Further, it also allows me to present some
of the thinking that went into conducting each subsequent study. So as
I introduce each study, I will explain some of the theoretical questions that
underlie it and hence suggest its importance. After presenting all three
studies and their results, I will discuss the implications of the entire set of
three studies, taken as a whole, for research on organizations and the world
of work.

STUDY 1
This study examines the relationships among three variables: polychronicity,
propensity for creativity, and orientation to change, with particular emphasis on polychronicity’s relationship with the other two variables. The study
employs a two-sample design to learn if the results obtained from the ﬁrst
sample can be replicated in the second.

Concepts and Questions
Orientation to Change
That change is ubiquitous in contemporary organizations and societies is a
cliché. So, it is not surprising that change holds a central place in major
theories of contemporary organizations. For example, environmental contingency theory posits that the more dynamic the environments are in which

Downloaded by University of Regina At 05:41 15 December 2016 (PT)

182

organizations operate, the more ﬂexible and changeable (i.e., organic) organizations need to be in order to experience high levels of effectiveness (Burns &
Stalker, 1961; Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967). Thus we can see that change occurs
twice in what may be the most enduring of organization theories.
Despite the theoretical and pragmatic centrality of change for modern
organizations, most textbooks containing extended discussions of organizational change also include discussions of resistance to change (e.g., Bateman
& Snell, 1999). Stanislao and Stanislao (1983) observed that resistance to
change seems to arise regardless of a change’s content. Indeed, Baldwin (1985)
once observed: ‘‘Most of us are about as eager to be changed as we were to be
born, and go through our changes in a similar state of shock’’ (p. 643).
Fortunately, variance in individual attitudes toward change in the workplace
has also been observed (e.g., Jacobson, Trumbo, Cheek, & Naugle, 1959;
Trumbo, 1961; Hardin, 1967). These observations suggest the existence of an
individual-level orientation to change variable, which is deﬁned here as the
extent of the individual’s overall preference for prospective and actual change
in the workplace. Like polychronicity, this variable is seen as a general traitlike characteristic of an individual’s personality, albeit little research has
been conducted on it. Obviously, resistance to change will have an important
impact on the outcome of organizational change efforts, so factors related to
it gain increased importance by that association. And polychronicity may be
one of the variables associated with orientation to change.
Hall and Hall (1990, p. 15) observed that highly polychronic people tend
to change plans more often and easily than less polychronic individuals. This
relationship suggests a general positive relationship between polychronicity
and orientation to change. Indeed, the polychronicity phenomenon is
usually characterized by a speciﬁc form of change: the degree of changing
back-and-forth among speciﬁc tasks. So, because polychronicity is regarded
as a trait-like personality variable (Slocombe & Bluedorn, 1999) that deals
with a form of change—switching back-and-forth between projects and
events—I developed the following research question:
Research Question 1: Are individuals’ polychronicity preferences and behaviors related
to their orientation to change, and if so, are the two variables positively correlated (i.e.,
the higher the polychronicity, the more receptive the individual is to change and the
more favorably the individual regards change)?

Propensity for Creativity
Creativity is a subset of organizational change (Woodman, Sawyer, &
Grifﬁn, 1993), and following work by Gough (1979) and others (Oldham &
Cummings, 1996), an individual’s propensity to be creative will be regarded

as a stable aspect of personality (i.e., as a general inclination to develop and
admire novel approaches to issues, problems, and situations). It seems to
follow, then, that polychronicity and propensity for creativity might be
positively correlated for two reasons. First, if polychronicity and orientation
to change are positively correlated, then polychronicity and propensity for
creativity should also be—if creativity is a speciﬁc form of change. Second,
the shifting back-and-forth between different projects by more polychronic
individuals ought to result in more cross-fertilization among their projects,
which would lead to more creativity as problem solutions from one project
migrate to others as solutions. These reasons lead to the following research
question:
Research Question 2: Are individuals’ polychronicity preferences and behaviors related
to their propensity for creativity, and if so, are the two variables positively correlated
(i.e., the higher the polychronicity, the greater propensity for creativity)?

And because creativity is a subset of change (Woodman et al., 1993), the
lines of thinking just presented suggest that propensity for creativity ought
to be related to an individual’s general orientation to change, which leads to
the following research question:
Research Question 3: Is a more favorable orientation to change positively correlated with
individuals’ propensities for creativity?

Method
Participants and Design
Data were collected with questionnaires from two samples. The data comprising Sample 1 were collected from 493 students in one graduate and four
undergraduate management classes at the University of Missouri-Columbia.
The data comprising Sample 2 were collected ﬁve months after the data in
Sample 1 were gathered, and were collected from 316 students in two large
undergraduate management courses at the University of Missouri-Columbia.1

Measures
The same measures were used in the questionnaires completed by the
respondents in both Sample 1 and Sample 2. Polychronicity was measured
with the individual version of the 10-item inventory of polychronic values
(IPV) (Bluedorn, Kalliath, Strube, & Martin, 1999); propensity for

Research Question 1 addressed a potential relationship between an individual’s level of polychronicity and the person’s general orientation to
change; and if a relationship existed, would the two variables be positively
correlated, as a number of reasons would lead one to anticipate. A signiﬁcant positive correlation occurred between the two variables (r ¼ .39,
pr.001 in both samples). Thus the two variables were positively correlated
in both samples.
Research Question 2 addressed a possible relationship between an individual’s level of polychronicity and propensity for creativity. A statistically signiﬁcant positive correlation was obtained in both samples (r ¼ .15, pr.001
and r ¼ .22, pr.001 in Samples 1 and 2, respectively). Thus polychronicity
and propensity for creativity were positively correlated in both samples.
The reasoning involved with Research Questions 1 and 2 suggested
orientation to change and propensity for creativity might be positively correlated, which led to Research Question 3 that asked if these two variables
were positively correlated. Statistically signiﬁcant positive correlations were
obtained (r ¼ .28, pr.001 and r ¼ .34, pr.001 in Samples 1 and 2, respectively) between the two variables.
To examine the robustness of the results just presented, all of which
were zero-order correlations, hierarchical regressions (not hierarchical linear
modeling)2 were conducted in both samples such that four variables—age,
gender, cumulative grade point average, and either propensity for creativity
or orientation to change as appropriate—were entered in the ﬁrst step, followed by polychronicity in the second. As revealed in Table 2, the relationship
between polychronicity and orientation to change revealed in the two zeroorder correlations in the two samples was supported by the hierarchical
regression results in both samples. The relationship between polychronicity
and propensity for creativity found in the zero-order correlations from both
samples, however, was only supported by the hierarchical regression analyses
in one of the two samples (Sample 2). Thus the results obtained with the zeroorder correlations between polychronicity and orientation to change were also
obtained with the hierarchical regression results in both samples, but the
results obtained with the zero-order correlations between polychronicity and
propensity for creativity were only partially supported (in Sample 2).

But the results were not as consistent regarding the relationship between
polychronicity and propensity for creativity. Although both the zero-order
correlations indicated a signiﬁcant positive relationship, the hierarchical
regression analyses provided stronger tests of this relationship, and they
produced mixed results. In the case of Sample 2, the hierarchical regression

positively correlated, but because this relationship was not the core issue
underlying this study (relationships with polychronicity were the core issue),
it was not explored further. But the theoretical and practical importance
of both orientation to change and propensity for creativity supports the
continued investigation of possible relationships between polychronicity and
these as well as other variables.

STUDY 2
The results from Study 1 revealed a relationship between polychronicity and
an important factor in the management of contemporary organizations:
an individual’s orientation to organizational change. Also supported, albeit
not as strongly, was a positive relationship between polychronicity and
propensity for creativity, which may be especially important when novelty
occurs in an organization’s environment. Moreover, new questions emerged
that I decided to investigate as well. One question involves the possibility
that individuals’ polychronicities will inﬂuence their perceptions of an organization’s attractiveness as an employer. The other question is a methodological
issue involved in testing the polychronicity–attractiveness relationship.
Organizational attractiveness is deﬁned, consistent with Turban and
Keon (1993), as current and prospective employees’ perceptions of how
attractive an organization is as a place to work. According to AttractionSelection-Attrition theory (Schneider, 1987; Schneider, Goldstein, & Smith,
1995) at least some personality traits should result in some organizations
appearing more attractive as potential employers than others. The idea that

Downloaded by University of Regina At 05:41 15 December 2016 (PT)

some organizations would look more attractive is also suggested by a
number of individual–organization congruence studies (e.g., Chatman,
1991; Meglino, Ravlin, & Adkins, 1989), including one on individual and
work-group polychronicity (Slocombe & Bluedorn, 1999).
Moreover, organizational attractiveness can be seen as a key link in the
chain of events that begins with factors leading an individual to accept a
position, and which ultimately ends in turnover. In between, a variety of
withdrawal behaviors can be observed that are often conceptualized as
forming a continuum ranging from relatively minor (reduced effort) to major
(turnover) forms of withdrawal (Melbin, 1961). One interpretation is that
when some forms of withdrawal are difﬁcult, withdrawal may be expressed
in one or more of the other forms (Price, 1977, p. 8). So, when employee
turnover is deﬁned in process terms as ‘‘the degree of individual movement
across the membership boundary of a social system’’ (Price, 1977, p. 4),
organizational attractiveness is clearly an important variable involved in the
part of the turnover process involving a person’s movement across the membership boundary into the organization. And given the effects of realistic job
previews on subsequent employee turnover (Wanous, 1973, 1980) as well as
this practice’s likely impact on the perceived attractiveness of an organization
as an employer, a potential relationship between polychronicity and organizational attractiveness could well have important theoretical and practical
implications.
That polychronicity is likely to be related to organizational attractiveness is
suggested by Conte and Jacobs’ (2003) ﬁndings that polychronicity was related
to other forms of withdrawal behaviors, speciﬁcally, tardiness and absenteeism
(the more polychronic the person, the more likely the person was to be absent
or tardy). Further, Slocombe and Bluedorn (1999) found that the more similar
individuals’ polychronicity levels were to the overall polychronicity levels of
their work units, the higher their levels were of organizational commitment,
which is a key variable in major models of the turnover process (e.g., Bluedorn,
1982). Given polychronicity’s relationship with other withdrawal behaviors
(absenteeism and tardiness) and at least one other variables in the turnover
process (organizational commitment), it is reasonable to extend research on
polychronicity to organizational attractiveness, which will be a consideration
to prospective employees very early in the overall turnover process, that is,
before a job is accepted or before, perhaps, a job is even sought in a speciﬁc
organization. This leads to the following research question:
Research Question 4: Is the similarity of individual and organizational polychronicity
related to the individual’s perception of an organization as an attractive place to work,
and if so, will the relationship be a positive correlation such that the greater the

similarity, the more attractive the organization will be perceived, and the lesser
similarity, the less attractive the organization will be perceived?

Failure to answer these questions afﬁrmatively would neither disconﬁrm
Attraction-Selection-Attrition theory (Schneider, 1987; Schneider et al., 1995)
nor the other research and theory cited because not every personality trait or
individual characteristic is a salient attraction or selection factor for either the
individual or the people making hiring decisions in an organization. Hence a
major agenda of research on individual and organization congruence includes
determining which individual and organizational characteristics result in
selection, attraction, attrition, and other outcomes, such as job satisfaction
and organizational commitment. Afﬁrmative answers to Research Question 4
would suggest that polychronicity is one of these characteristics.
This question was examined by presenting two scenarios to each respondent, which makes the order of scenario presentation a factor that could affect
the results. Therefore, the study design varied the order in which the two
scenarios were presented, which allows the effects of scenario order to be
examined, to answer the following methodological question:
Methodological Question 1: Does the order in which the scenarios are presented to
respondents affect the respondents’ perceptions of how attractive the organizations
depicted in the scenarios are as a place to work, and by that affect the relationship
between polychronicity and organizational attractiveness?

In addition to examining Research Question 4 and Methodological Question
1, I decided to include tests of the relationships between polychronicity and
both propensity for creativity and orientation to change that were found in
both samples in Study 1. The tests, however, will be more rigorous than those
conducted in Study 1. The greater rigor will stem from the addition of four
more control variables to the hierarchical regressions—locus of control,
which is ‘‘a generalized expectancy that rewards, reinforcements, or outcomes
in life are controlled either by one’s own actions (internality) or by other
forces (externality)’’ (Spector, 1988, p. 335); tolerance for ambiguity, which is
‘‘a range, from rejection to attraction, of reactions to stimuli perceived as
unfamiliar, complex, dynamically uncertain, or subject to multiple conﬂicting
interpretations’’ (McClain, 1994, p. 184); propensity for creativity; and orientation to change. Locus of control was added as a control variable because
it was associated with orientation to change in other research (Dunham,
Grube, Gardner, Cummings, & Pierce, 1989); tolerance for ambiguity was
added because Oldham and Cummings (1996) noted it has been associated
with creativity in previous research, and it has also been related empirically to
orientation to change (Dunham et al., 1989); and both orientation to change

results?

Method
Participants and Design
Students in the same two undergraduate management classes from which the
data were collected that formed the second sample in Study 1 were offered the
opportunity to take part in an extension of that project. In return for this
participation they would receive extra credit amounting to approximately
1 percent of their total grade. Of the students who elected to take part,
248 had also completed a questionnaire in the second sample of Study 1.
These 248 students are the sample for this study, and the combined data
from the two questionnaires—which were matched through the use of identiﬁcation numbers3—completed by these respondents comprise the data for
this study.
Combining the data from the two questionnaires administered nine days
apart allows for the examination of the relationships among several variables while minimizing the effects of common source and method variance

This study focused on examining Research Question 4 to see if polychronicity was related to organizational attractiveness, and if so, if the more
polychronic the individual, the more attractive the person would perceive an
organization to be in which polychronic behaviors were frequently displayed
and were highly valued. And conversely, if the more polychronic the individual, the less attractive the person would perceive an organization to be in
which a low amount of polychronic behaviors were displayed, and in which

examination of the zero-order correlations between polychronicity and the

to maintain consistency across all analyses, two-tailed tests were used to

Additional Findings
Tolerance for ambiguity was included in this study because other research
indicated it might be related to orientation to change (Dunham et al., 1989),
suggesting a proper role for it as a control variable. It revealed, however,
statistically signiﬁcant zero-order correlations with polychronicity measured

after controlling for the effects of several other variables. And the relationships persisted. After controlling for age, GPA, gender, propensity for creativity, locus of control, and orientation to change, polychronicity measured
at Time 1 revealed a beta of .15 (pr.01), and polychronicity measured at

Discussion
The results concerning organizational attractiveness indicate polychronicity
may be a factor on which individuals choose the organizations they select
as their employers. By using two scenarios, one depicting a high level of
polychronicity, the other a low level, two mutually reinforcing tests were
conducted of this possibility. As with orientation to change, both the zeroorder correlation analysis and the hierarchical regressions revealed statistically signiﬁcant relationships in the anticipated directions, relationships
that persisted after controlling for several important variables and a methodological factor. Should these results be replicated in another sample,
polychronicity deserves to be studied as a possible selection factor in the
attraction-selection-attrition process. So the answers to Research Question 4
are that in this sample the polychronicity of individuals and the perceived
polychronicity displayed in organizations are related to individuals’ perceptions of organizational attractiveness such that the more similar the individual
and the individual’s perception of the level of polychronicity in an organization, the more attractive the organization appears to be as a place to work
as a manager.
As for Research Question 5, polychronicity had a signiﬁcant positive
relationship (correlation) with orientation to change, a relationship that
persisted not only after controlling for several individual characteristics as in
Study 1, but after controlling for several important individual characteristics
and personality traits. Moreover, the same substantive results were obtained
using either of the two measures of orientation to change included in
the study. These results lend considerable support to the conclusion that
polychronicity and orientation to change are positively related consistently,
and that the relationship is not a spurious correlation or a methodological
artifact. This is especially so because these tests were conducted four times:
with polychronicity measured at Time 1 and at Time 2, and with change
measured with both the modiﬁed Trumbo and the Dunham et al. (1989)
scales.

The same conclusion, however, cannot be drawn about the relationship
between polychronicity and propensity for creativity. This relationship was
supported at the zero-order level, but it was reduced to nonsignﬁcance after
controlling for the effects of several other variables, including orientation
to change and tolerance for ambiguity that had substantially larger zeroorder correlations with propensity for creativity than polychronicity. This
suggests, depending on the causal ordering among the variables, that the
correlation between polychronicity and propensity for creativity is either
spurious or largely mediated by one or more control variables. Thus in
answer to Research Question 5, the relationship between polychronicity and
orientation to change was replicated, and after introducing control variables,
the relationship between polychronicity and propensity for creativity repeated
that found in the Sample 1 results of Study 1.
An unanticipated ﬁnding was the emergence of tolerance for ambiguity as
a variable with which polychronicity was consistently related, both at the
level of zero-order correlations and in multiple regression equations after
controlling for the effects of several important variables. Having emerged
unexpectedly from these data, this relationship is an important candidate for
a replication effort.
Methodologically, although scenario order (Methodological Question 1)
affected the perceived attractiveness of the polychronicity scenario, when
entered as a ﬁnal control variable, all of the relationships persisted between
polychronicity and orientation to change, organizational attractiveness, and
tolerance for ambiguity; and they persisted without substantive changes.
Moreover, all of the results were substantively similar regardless of whether
polychronicity was measured at Time 1 or Time 2, which indicates the substantive results obtained in Study 1 were not the results of a common method,
common time effect. As a ﬁnal methodological note, the presence of the IPV
in both the Time 1 and Time 2 questionnaires allowed a test of the IPV’s test–
retest reliability across nine days by calculating the correlation between the
two scores. This correlation was r ¼ .73 (pr.001).

With one major exception, the purpose of Study 3 was to see if the ﬁndings of
Study 2 regarding orientation to change, propensity for creativity, tolerance
for ambiguity, and organizational attractiveness could be replicated in a
different sample. The exception was the addition of a second methodological
treatment: the positioning of the IPV, the measure of polychronicity, before

or after the two scenarios that described organizations whose managers
displayed high and low levels of polychronicity. Thus Study 3 focused on one
research question and one methodological question:
Research Question 6: Will the relationships between polychronicity and orientation to
change, propensity to creativity, tolerance for ambiguity, and organizational attractiveness
be replicated in a new sample?
Methodological Question 3: Will the relationships between polychronicity and orientation
to change, propensity to creativity, tolerance for ambiguity, and organizational attractiveness be changed (a) by the scenario ordering, (b) if the IPV precedes or follows the two
scenarios, or (c) by an interaction between scenario ordering and whether the IPV precedes
or follows the two scenarios?

Method
Participants and Design
As were most of the samples in the ﬁrst two studies, the sample for Study 3
was drawn from students in a large undergraduate management course at
the University of Missouri-Columbia. The students in the course were given
the opportunity to earn extra credit (approximately 1 percent of their course
grade) by participating in this study. The project required attendance at an
administration of the questionnaire during a group session held in the
evening rather than during class time. As a result of the offer, 364 students
participated and completed questionnaires.

The ﬁnal points of interest in this study are methodological (Methodological Question 3). One of the issues is the question of scenario ordering,
which was initially investigated in Study 2. The second issue is the possible
inﬂuence of measuring polychronicity before or after the respondent is presented with the scenarios and the measures of their attractiveness. The ﬁnal
aspect is the possible interaction of the scenario ordering with the before or
after presentation of the polychronicity measure. Because two independent
variables (scenario ordering and before or after presentation of the IPV) and
three dependent variables (attractiveness of the low and high polychronicity
scenarios, polychronicity) would most plausibly be involved, a MANOVA
was conducted to see if signiﬁcant effects were present among these variables.

The MANOVA revealed the presence of signiﬁcant effects for scenario
ordering, Wilks’ l(3, 350) ¼ .89, pr.001, and for presenting the IPV before
or after the scenarios, Wilks’ l(3, 350) ¼ .98, pr.01, but not for the scenario
ordering X IPV position interaction term, Wilks’ l(3, 350) ¼ .99, n.s. So
I proceeded to two-way ANOVAs (Rosenthal & Rosnow, 1991) for both
scenario order and IPV position, but not for their interactions. The two-way

for the attractiveness of the high polychronicity scenario: F(1, 359) ¼ 24.18,

in both Study 2 and this study, and as in Study 2, the high polychronicity scenario was perceived as more attractive when it followed the low
polychronicity scenario rather than preceding it.
The two-way ANOVAs also revealed that presenting the polychronicity
measure before or after the scenarios produced a statistically signiﬁcant
association with the polychronicity (IPV) score: F(1, 355) ¼ 9.13, pr.003,
Z ¼ .16. Polychronicity scores were higher when the IPV was positioned
after the scenarios rather than before them. Whether the IPV was positioned
before or after the scenarios had no statistically signiﬁcant effects on the
perception of the attractiveness of the scenarios: F(1, 360) ¼ .38, n.s., for
the low polychronicity scenario; and F(1, 359) ¼ 1.81, n.s., for the high
polychronicity scenario.
Thus the two-way ANOVAs detected two main effects: of scenario
ordering on the high polychronicity scenario’s attractiveness and on the
polychronicity score by positioning the IPV either before or after the two
scenarios. Given these results and to repeat and extend the results from
Study 2, hierarchical regression analyses were conducted again. As presented in Tables 7 and 8 the results of these analyses revealed little or no

Discussion
This study generally replicated the results obtained in Study 2 (Research
Question 6). Polychronicity was positively related to both orientation to
change and to tolerance for ambiguity in tests made with zero-order correlations. With hierarchical regression tests, polychronicity’s relationship
with orientation to change was reduced to a nonsigniﬁcant beta when the
Dunham et al. (1989) measure of orientation to change was used, but it
continued to produce a signiﬁcant positive beta when the modiﬁed Trumbo
(1961) measure was used in the analyses. The polychronicity–tolerance for
ambiguity relationship continued to produce a signiﬁcant positive beta in
the hierarchical regression analyses. These relationships generally replicated
those obtained in Study 2, indicating an increased generality beyond a single
sample, by that increasing conﬁdence in their existence beyond tests of statistical signiﬁcance. Also repeated were the respective negative and positive
relationships with the attractiveness of the low and high polychronicity
scenarios. These relationships, too, were found in zero-order correlations
and in hierarchical regression after the effects of several demographic and
psychological control variables had been taken into account. As such, they
replicate the results obtained in Study 2. The negative results for propensity
for creativity were replicated too because the relationship between polychronicity and propensity for creativity did not persist in the face of the
statistical controls employed. And all of these results persisted—for scenario
attractiveness, tolerance for ambiguity, propensity for creativity, and orientation to change—after the two methodological factors were added to the
hierarchical regressions (the core issue in Methodological Question 3). These
replications and the persistence of the relationships after the addition of
methodological controls strengthens conﬁdence in the ﬁndings, several of
which have important theoretical ramiﬁcations that will be discussed in the
next section.

GENERAL DISCUSSION
Before 1990, very little (e.g., Bluedorn & Denhardt, 1988; Schein, 1985) had
been written let alone studied about polychronicity beyond Hall’s (1959,
1983) work on the subject, and outside of Hall’s work, one is hard put to
ﬁnd anything empirical beyond Haase, Lee, and Banks’ (1979) early effort.
Compared to this short bibliography, research cited in this article (e.g.,
Bluedorn, 1999; Conte & Gintoft, 2005; Conte & Jacobs, 2003; Hecht &
Allen, 2005; Kaufman et al., 1991; Slocombe & Bluedorn, 1999; Tinsley,
1998) reveals growing interest in this variable. To the breadth provided by
this burgeoning research stream, the set of three studies presented in this
article adds much needed depth. The investigation of any new phenomenon
is likely to generate interest in its possible connection to a wider range of
phenomena, and the recent investigation of polychronicity is no exception.
This is not bad, especially if the breadth can be complemented with some
depth as well. Providing some of that depth as well as investigating
some important methodological issues were the motivation behind the set of
three studies presented here.
Heretofore, no direct investigations had ever been conducted on polychronicity’s relationships with orientation to change, propensity for creativity,
organizational attractiveness, or tolerance for ambiguity, the latter, admittedly, being a serendipitous discovery.4 The results produced by these three
studies not only establish the existence of polychronicity’s association with
orientation to change, tolerance for ambiguity, and organizational attractiveness, they demonstrate these relationships persist after controlling for
several major individual characteristics and personality variables. The results
from these studies reveal that although scores for individual variables may
have been affected by methodological machinations, the relationships among
the variables of interest in this research were not. Moreover, the relationships
persisted in form (sign), statistical signiﬁcance, and general magnitude (with
only the exception of the nonsigniﬁcant beta for polychronicity in Study 3
when orientation to change was measured with the Dunham et al., 1989 scale)
regardless of whether all variables were measured on the same questionnaire
or were measured on two questionnaires completed nine days apart. And
with the single exception just noted, they persisted this way too if different
operationalizations of orientation to change were used. Despite all of the
factors that might have reduced or eliminated the relationships, they persisted; in the case of polychronicity and orientation to change, in seven out of
eight hierarchical regressions (including two out of three with orientation to
change measured with the Dunham et al. scale) across four samples; in the

Downloaded by University of Regina At 05:41 15 December 2016 (PT)

210

ALLEN C. BLUEDORN

case of tolerance for ambiguity and organizational attractiveness, in all six
hierarchical regressions across two samples. This leads to conﬁdence, indeed,
to the expectation that in future studies (1) polychronicity will be positively
correlated with orientation to change; (2) polychronicity will be positively
correlated with tolerance for ambiguity; and (3) polychronicity will be related
to the attractiveness of potential employer organizations: positively to those
organizations whose employees display high levels of polychronicity, negatively
to those whose employees display low amounts.
These results are important theoretically because they help us understand
the nexus of variables, the nomological net (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955)
of which polychronicity is a part. Knowing that polychronicity is tightly
intertwined with individuals’ orientations to change and their tolerances for
ambiguity is suggestive of an important complex of personality characteristics that may be more receptive to the change demands associated with
working in contemporary organizations and their environments. This suggests that highly polychronic individuals with high tolerances for ambiguity
and favorable orientations to change may be more receptive to, or at least
more comfortable with, the amount of change encountered by employees in
organic organizations and the dynamic environments in which they operate.
The favorable orientation to change would obviously lead to a greater
degree of comfort with constant change, the high tolerance for ambiguity
would be helpful in addressing novelty in the changes, and higher levels of
polychronicity would facilitate the ability to constantly add new tasks to
one’s set of activities as new changes emerge.
Despite the solid support as well as the theoretical importance of the
relationships just discussed, one potential relationship received scant support, the relationship between polychronicity and propensity for creativity.
Although polychronicity was positively correlated with propensity for creativity in several of the studies, all of these correlations were zero-order
correlations, and once statistical controls were introduced, all but one of
these relationship shrank in magnitude and became statistically nonsigniﬁcant. This suggests there is no relationship between the two variables. In the
studies I report here, however, the evidence for this conclusion does not
directly address the question of whether polychronicity is related to creativity itself. The evidence from my studies deals with the related but different
question of whether polychronicity is related to a propensity for creativity in
an individual’s personality.
Recent research has more directly tested the question of whether a person’s polychronicity is related to a person’s actual creativity. This question
was addressed in a laboratory experiment (Hecht, 2002) that varied the

Downloaded by University of Regina At 05:41 15 December 2016 (PT)

211

polychronicity demands of a work situation and also measured participants’
general polychronicity with a modiﬁed and augmented version of the IPV
(the same polychronicity scale I used in the studies reported here). The creative task was an individual brainstorming exercise (uses for a pipecleaner)
that could be evaluated and measured quantitatively (the more distinct uses,
the greater the creativity). Hecht found a modest but statistically signiﬁcant
positive correlation between individuals’ general polychronicity and how
much creativity they displayed on the task as measured by the number of distinct uses for a pipecleaner each participant produced. The similarity between
the level of polychronicity demanded in the task context (manipulated by the
experimenter) and the individual’s polychronicity, however, did not inﬂuence
the level of creativity exhibited in the task. Because Hecht’s study involved the
direct examination of creativity, its results can be regarded as a stronger test
of polychronicity’s relationship with creativity than the results reported in this
article. This is because my results would require at least one more inference
than Hecht’s; to wit, that people with high propensities for creativity will
actually be more creative in their work and lives. Hecht’s design did not
require such an inference because she measured creativity directly. So there
may be a modest relationship between polychronicity and creativity after all.
As for organizational attractiveness, an individual’s level of polychronicity was consistently correlated with how polychronic an organization’s
employees appeared to be based on two scenarios: one written to represent
an organization in which people behaved with a high level of polychronicity
and the other written to represent an organization in which people behaved
with a low level of polychronicity. This was the sole purpose of the scenarios. The results indicate there is a relationship, but they are silent on the
issue of how important polychronicity is as a factor in organizational
attractiveness compared to the almost inﬁnite number of other variables
that could characterize an organization. Addressing this issue is a logical
next step for research on this issue.
Indeed, a speciﬁc question such research might address is whether the
organization as a whole, actually perceptions of it, matter at all. The results
presented in this article certainly indicate that perceptions of the organization matter, but how much does the organization matter compared to the
position in it the individual is considering? It is possible that both matter,
that they might even interact statistically, leading to research questions, such
as which situation would a highly polychronic person perceive more favorably: (1) a position in which the position holder is expected to behave very
polychronically, but is embedded in what is seen to be a very monochromic
organization; or (2) a position in which the person is expected to behave

Downloaded by University of Regina At 05:41 15 December 2016 (PT)

212

ALLEN C. BLUEDORN

very monochronically, but which is embedded in what is seen to be as a very
polychronic organization as a whole? In other words, how much does the
position and the individual’s perceptions of the behavioral norms associated
with the position affect the individual’s judgment about how attractive the
organization would be as a place to work, and how much does the rest of the
context involved with that position matter? Obviously, we would expect a
position associated with polychronic norms located in what is perceived to
be a very polychronic organization to be most favorably perceived as a place
of employment by a very polychronic individual—and just the opposite for a
position associated with monochronic norms in what is seen as a very monochronic organization—but it is unclear how to predict the attractiveness of the
two mixed-case combinations.
Making the situation even more complex, but perhaps more realistic,
would be the individual’s expectations about moving into other positions in
the focal organization. If the organization has a reputation for providing
rapid advancement or having career structures involving experience in many
positions, perhaps the position associated with monochronic norms in an
organization viewed as polychronic might be seen as the more attractive
situation than the opposite combination, especially for individuals with relatively long future temporal depths (horizons). But that is another topic
(Bluedorn, 2002) as well as another potential contingency.
Yet these examples of mixed cases suggest other situations. For example,
a general positive correlation was found between polychronicity and an
individual’s orientation to change in all four samples, indicating that more
polychronic people are more favorably oriented to change. But how attractive would a position be to a relatively polychronic individual if the position
were associated with polychronic norms in what is seen as a static, relatively
unchanging organization? How attractive would the opposite combination
be? The results from all three studies indicate polychronicity and orientation
to change co-vary in the same direction, but what happens when they move in
opposite directions? These are intriguing questions that seem very promising
for future research.
But another, equally fundamental question also needs to be addressed, and
that question is, by what means and cues does a person perceive an organization as having people who behave with high or low levels of polychronicity? After a person is employed, there is direct contact and observation, but
are perceptions made on this factor before crossing the membership boundary
into the organization? And if they are, what are the cues the individual uses to
reach such judgments? Other questions arise as well. In two of the studies
I presented in this article, polychronicity information about a potential

Downloaded by University of Regina At 05:41 15 December 2016 (PT)

employer was explicitly written and provided to respondents. In everyday life,
such information will usually be much more subtle, even tacit. If so, can it
be detected by either party—the job seeker or the organization through its
representatives—prior to an individual joining an organization? If it can be
detected, then the polychronicity–attractiveness ﬁndings are likely to generalize to the attraction and selection phases of the attraction-selection-attrition
process. But if detection requires more sustained contact with the organization, the polychronicity–attractiveness ﬁndings may be more relevant to the
attrition phase of this process.
These are intriguing and challenging questions to investigate, and answers
to them may well provide information, not just about polychronicity, but
also about the key characteristics that make an organization attractive as
well as the cues individuals use to make inferences about organizations
before joining them.
But regarding the ﬁndings themselves in the studies I have reported here,
despite the large number of statistical controls that generated these results,
and despite the multiple replications of them, are these relationships general
rules of human behavior? Though large, the samples gathered in all the
studies were drawn from the undergraduate population that was enrolled,
with the exception of one class, in undergraduate management courses at the
University of Missouri-Columbia, albeit over a period of two years. And it
may be a long way from the American Midwest to Tipperary. Stated
differently, are the variables that distinguish the population from which
these samples were drawn from other populations (e.g., organizational context, age, ethnic composition, educational level, vocational interests, etc.)
also likely to affect the relationships identiﬁed, supported, and replicated in
this series of studies? Undergraduate students are real people, after all, so
there is no inherent reason to believe the relationships supported so well in
this research would automatically disappear if they were studied in samples
drawn from other populations.
Therefore, given the relative thoroughness of their examination in the
studies presented in this article, the next reasonable step in their investigation is to do so in samples drawn from populations that differ from this one
in important ways. One possibility would be a cross-cultural investigation in
which the same methods and scales were used with samples of management
students drawn from outside the United States. Another possibility would
be a sample drawn from a population of managers and other employees
in American organizations, or more ambitiously, drawn from workforces in
several countries, countries that might be selected strategically in order to
maximize important cultural differences. Whatever possibilities are explored,

APPENDIX
In this appendix, I describe the measures of the variables included in the
several studies reported in this article. To organize this presentation, I group
the variables into two categories: individual demographic and academic
characteristics, and psychometric measures.

Individual Demographic and Academic Characteristics
Three individual demographic and academic characteristics were measured in
these studies: the respondent’s age, gender, and grade point average. All three
were measured with self-report items directing the respondents to report their
age (responded by writing the age on a blank line), gender (responded by
circling a number for either male or female), and cumulative grade point
average (responded by writing the grade point average on a blank line).

Psychometric Measures
Six variables were measured psychometrically: polychronicity, locus of
control, organizational attractiveness, orientation to change, propensity for
creativity, and tolerance for ambiguity.
Polychronicity
Polychronicity was measured with the individual version of the IPV, whose
development and psychometric validation was presented thoroughly in
Bluedorn et al. (1999). The IPV is a 10-item Likert-type scale, and individuals

Downloaded by University of Regina At 05:41 15 December 2016 (PT)

Polychronicity, Individuals, and Organizations

219

respond to each statement on a seven-point scale where 7 ¼ strongly agree
and 1 ¼ strongly disagree. Each of the ten statements describes either the
respondent’s beliefs (e.g., ‘‘I believe people should try to do many things
at once’’) or preferences (e.g., ‘‘I would rather complete parts of several
projects every day than complete an entire project’’). Five of the items are
worded to indicate a preference for a high level of polychronicity, and the
other ﬁve are worded to indicate a preference for a low level of polychronicity
(monochronicity). To score the entire scale, the ﬁve items that indicate a
preference for a low level of polychronicity are reverse scored and then all 10
items are summed and divided by 10. Dividing by 10 converts the IPV scores
to the original seven-item response scale, and the higher the score, the more
the individual prefers to act polychronically. As reported in Bluedorn et al.
(1999) and other studies (e.g., Bluedorn & Ferris, 2004), both the individual
and group versions of the IPV reveal strong psychometric properties.
Locus of Control
Locus of control was measured with the achievement component of Lefcourt’s
(1981) multidimensional-multiattribution causality scale. The achievement
component is a Likert-type scale consisting of 24 items that individuals
respond to on a ﬁve-point scale ranging from 0 ¼ disagree to 4 ¼ agree. This
24-item scale was designed to deal with achievement attributions by undergraduate students in a college environment, so it was developed for use by
exactly the same kinds of participants who took part in the studies reported in
this article (almost entirely undergraduate college students). The scale includes
items that measure the traditional internal attributions of effort and ability as
well as the traditional external attributions of luck and context. The scale and
its subscales can be scored and used in a variety of ways. In these studies it was
scored so it could range from 0 to 96 so that the higher the score, the more
external the respondent’s attributions were for academic performance and
outcomes.
Organizational Attractiveness
Suggested by Turban and Keon’s work (1993, p. 188), a two-item Likert-type
organizational attractiveness scale was developed comprised of the following
two items: ‘‘To what extent would you enjoy being a manager in Organization
A?’’ and ‘‘If you had job offers from ﬁve companies, one of which was
Organization A, and all offered the same salary, how likely would you be to
accept the offer from Organization A?’’ Participants responded to each item
by choosing a response on a seven-point scale. For the ﬁrst question, the

to be a manager and 7 ¼ I can’t imagine a better place to be a manager. The

Propensity for Creativity
Propensity for creativity was measured with Gough’s (1979) CPS as modiﬁed slightly by Oldham and Cummings (1996, p. 615). The CPS consists of
30 adjectives (Oldham and Cummings substituted ‘‘phony’’ for ‘‘affected’’
in the list), and respondents were asked to place a check mark in front of
each adjective they believed described themselves and to leave blank the
adjectives that did not describe them. Some of the adjectives (18) in the list
are considered descriptive of creative people (e.g., insightful, original)
whereas 12 of the adjectives are considered descriptive of less creative people
(e.g., commonplace, submissive). Basically, the number of ‘‘less creative’’
adjectives checked is subtracted from the number of ‘‘creative’’ adjectives
checked to obtain the respondent’s score, which could range from 12 to 18.
To calculate the reliability (alpha) statistic for this scale, I followed
the weighted composite technique used by Oldham and Cummings (1996,
p. 616).
Orientation to Change
Two different scales were used to measure the respondents’ orientation to
change, one developed by Trumbo (1961) and the other by Dunham et al.
(1989).
The Trumbo (1961) Scale. The original Trumbo scale was modiﬁed by

student sample (‘‘It would take a sizeable raise in pay to get me to voluntarily transfer to another job’’), it was not included in the scale. Second,
analyses of data from Sample 1 revealed that the removal of two additional
items (Items 1 and 5 as presented in Trumbo, 1961), substantially increased
the scale’s alpha coefﬁcient to .74 in this sample. Removing these two items
produced a principal components analysis solution revealing only a single
factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1 (2.69, which explained 44.8 percent

The Dunham et al. (1989) Scale.
The Dunham et al. (1989) scale is an 18-item Likert-type scale containing
three six-item subscales: one each for affective (e.g., ‘‘I ﬁnd most change to
be pleasing’’), behavioral (e.g., ‘‘I usually resist new ideas’’), and cognitive
(e.g., ‘‘I usually beneﬁt from change’’) aspects of an individual’s attitude or
orientation to change. In the studies reported in this article, I used a sevenpoint response scale for each item anchored as follows: 1 ¼ Strongly disagree and 7 ¼ Strongly agree. For each of the three subscales, after the
appropriate reverse scoring, the six items were summed and the sum was
divided by 6. For the scale as a whole, after the appropriate reverse scoring,
the 18 items were summed, and the sum was divided by 18. Thus in all cases,
the range of possible scores was 1–7, with higher scores always indicating a
more favorable orientation to change.

respondents how well they deal with novelty and ambiguity (e.g., ‘‘I prefer
familiar situations to new ones,’’ McClain, 1993, p. 187). For the studies
